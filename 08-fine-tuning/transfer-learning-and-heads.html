Transfer Learning & Heads

Your side of the pretrain/adapt economy begins. This module is the deep dive you asked for on fine-tuning — six pages from the fundamental move to the decision framework. Page one: why a model trained on the internet can learn YOUR task from 500 examples, what a "head" is, what "freezing" means, and the failure mode (catastrophic forgetting) that shapes every recipe that follows.

The fundamental move: new head, borrowed body

A pretrained encoder ends in rich contextual vectors; a pretrained decoder ends in next-token logits. Neither ends in your labels. Fine-tuning's basic move: chop off the model's final layer, bolt on a fresh small "head" for your task (for classification: one M1.3 matrix mapping d → num_classes), and train on your data. The body arrives knowing language (M6.1's expensive gift); the head learns to read your labels off representations that already contain almost everything needed.

The freezing dial: three settings

"Freezing" = excluding parameters from training (no gradients, no optimizer state — which, per M7.2's bill, also means huge memory savings). The dial has three classic positions: feature extraction — freeze everything, train only the head; the body is a fixed featurizer (you did exactly this in M3.7's Build 4: MiniLM + logistic regression). Full fine-tuning — everything trainable; best quality, most expensive, most dangerous (forgetting, below). Partial/graded — freeze bottom layers, tune top ones; or use layer-wise LR decay (smaller learning rates for deeper layers), respecting the heat-map: let each layer move in proportion to how task-specific it should be. (Position four on the dial — LoRA — gets its own page, 8.5.)

Catastrophic forgetting: the price of plasticity

The failure mode that stalks every aggressive fine-tune: gradient descent has no loyalty to old knowledge. Weights encode pretraining's competence; your task's gradients happily overwrite them if pushed hard (high LR, many epochs, narrow data). Symptoms: the model aces your task but its general abilities degrade — an LLM fine-tuned hard on JSON extraction starts writing stilted prose; a chat model tuned on medical text forgets how to refuse. Defenses, all reducible to "move less": small LR (2e-5, not 1e-3), few epochs, mixed data (blend some general text into task data), freezing/LoRA (structurally limit what can move), and evaluation on general benchmarks, not just your task, before shipping.

Real numbers

The sample-efficiency miracle, quantified on sentiment classification: from-scratch LSTM needs ~25k labeled examples for 85% accuracy; fine-tuned DistilBERT hits ~90% with 500; even 50 examples gets ~84% — because the body's "is this text positive-ish?" features already exist and merely need wiring to labels. That 50× data reduction is transfer learning's entire commercial value: labeled data is the scarcest resource in applied ML, and pretraining is a subsidy on it.

Connect the dots

You've already run two settings of the freezing dial: M3.7's Build 4 was feature extraction (frozen MiniLM + trained LR head — and it beat TF-IDF by 5 points); GLiNER (M10 soon) is a pretrained encoder + a clever span-matching head — this page's pattern with a fancier hat. And the heat-map explains a fact you'll use in M9: sentence-embedding fine-tuning mostly reshapes upper layers' geometry, which is why even light contrastive tuning transforms retrieval quality without touching the model's language core.

Build: measure the freezing dial yourself
import torch, torch.nn as nn
from transformers import AutoModel, AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
body = AutoModel.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

# the head transplant, literally:
head = nn.Linear(384, 3)                      # your 3 classes
print("body params:", sum(p.numel() for p in body.parameters()))   # 22.7M
print("head params:", sum(p.numel() for p in head.parameters()))   # 1,155

# setting 1: feature extraction — freeze the body
for p in body.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False
trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in body.parameters() if p.requires_grad) \
          + sum(p.numel() for p in head.parameters())
print(f"trainable now: {trainable:,} ({trainable/22.7e6:.3%} of the model)")

# setting 3: unfreeze just the top 2 of 6 layers
for name, p in body.named_parameters():
    p.requires_grad = ("encoder.layer.4" in name or "encoder.layer.5" in name)
trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in body.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
print(f"top-2-layers setting: {trainable:,} trainable in body")

Three print statements, and "freezing" stops being jargon: it's requires_grad = False, and you just watched it move the trainable count from 22.7M to 1,155 to ~7M. Wire this into M3.7's training loop with your own labels and you have a production-pattern classifier this afternoon.

Interview questions

Why does fine-tuning need so much less data than training from scratch?

Pretraining already learned the hard part — general language features (syntax, semantics, world knowledge) occupying 99.9%+ of parameters. Fine-tuning only learns a mapping from those existing features to task labels (plus light feature adjustment in upper layers). Learning a d→classes head from rich representations is a low-sample problem; learning language from scratch is not. Hence 500 examples ≈ competitive where scratch training needs 25k+ — transfer is a data subsidy.

Feature extraction vs full fine-tuning — trade-offs and when to choose each?

Feature extraction (frozen body): minutes to train, tiny memory (no body gradients/optimizer — M7.2), zero forgetting risk, one body can serve many heads; ceiling limited by how well generic features fit the task. Full fine-tuning: adapts representations themselves, typically several points better, especially for domain-shifted data; costs full training memory, risks forgetting, one model copy per task. Heuristic: start frozen (cheap baseline), go full/partial/LoRA only if the quality gap on YOUR eval justifies it.

What is catastrophic forgetting, mechanically, and how do you mitigate it?

Gradient descent optimizes only the current loss; nothing preserves weights that encoded pretraining competence, so aggressive task training overwrites them — task metrics rise while general capability quietly degrades. Mitigations: lower LR and fewer epochs (smaller total weight movement), freeze lower layers or use LoRA (structurally cap movement), mix general data into the fine-tuning set (replay), and — the operational must — evaluate general behavior, not just task metrics, before shipping. For LLMs, KL-regularization to the base model (M8.4's leash) is the same idea formalized.

Why do lower transformer layers change less during fine-tuning?

Empirically (probing + weight-delta studies): lower layers encode general, task-agnostic patterns (morphology, local syntax — M5.6's hierarchy) useful for any objective, so task gradients demand little change there; upper layers encode abstract, task-relevant semantics where adaptation pays. Gradients naturally concentrate change where loss reduction is available. Practitioners exploit this via layer-wise LR decay and bottom-freezing — aligning the training procedure with where change is needed.

Your fine-tuned model wins on the task but users say it "got weirder." What happened and what's your process fix?

Classic catastrophic forgetting / distribution narrowing: the tune over-specialized (too many epochs, high LR, or homogeneous data), degrading general fluency, format flexibility, or safety behavior — invisible to your task-only eval. Fixes: retrain with gentler recipe + mixed data; adopt LoRA (bounded change, instantly revertible); and process-wise, add a general-capability regression suite (held-out chat/format/QA probes) to the ship gate alongside task metrics. The insight interviewers want: your eval defines what you protect — anything unmeasured is negotiable to SGD.
