The Fine-tune Recipe

The practitioner's page: everything between "I have data" and "I have a model I trust," in the order it actually matters. Spoiler with two decades of consensus behind it: your data quality decides 80% of the outcome; hyperparameters decide 15%; everything else is 5%. We'll spend the page in those proportions — and finish with the skill of reading training curves like a clinician.

Data first: the 80%

The checklist that outperforms any hyperparameter search: Consistency — are labels applied by the same rules everywhere? (Have two people label 50 examples independently; their disagreement rate is your model's ceiling.) Deduplication — near-duplicates inflate confidence and leak across splits (M3.3's sin). Coverage — does the data span the real input distribution, including the ugly cases? A model only generalizes across what it saw varied. Format hygiene — for generative tunes, the format IS part of the behavior being learned; inconsistent formatting teaches inconsistency. The split discipline — carve out validation and test BEFORE looking at anything (temporal split if your data has time). Every hour here beats a day of tuning.

Grid-searching five knobs on 2k examples is how you overfit the validation set (M3.3's leakage-by-experimenter) while feeling productive.

Reading curves like a clinician

Checkpoints, seeds, and honesty

Three habits that separate reliable results from lucky ones: load_best_model_at_end — evaluate every epoch, keep the best-validation checkpoint, not the last (automated early stopping). Seeds — small-data fine-tunes vary ±1–2 points run-to-run from initialization and shuffling alone; any claimed improvement smaller than your seed-variance is noise (run 3 seeds before believing a win — M3.7's last interview question, now a rule). The untouched test set — one number, at the very end, after all decisions. That's the number you report.

Build (capstone A): fine-tune, break, and heal
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import (AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification,
                          TrainingArguments, Trainer)
import numpy as np

ds = load_dataset("rotten_tomatoes")
small = ds["train"].shuffle(seed=42).select(range(2000))     # realistic scarcity
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilbert-base-uncased")
def tokenize(b): return tok(b["text"], truncation=True, padding="max_length", max_length=128)
train = small.map(tokenize, batched=True)
val   = ds["validation"].map(tokenize, batched=True)

model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("distilbert-base-uncased", num_labels=2)

args = TrainingArguments("ft-demo", learning_rate=2e-5, num_train_epochs=6,  # 6 on purpose!
    per_device_train_batch_size=16, eval_strategy="epoch", save_strategy="epoch",
    load_best_model_at_end=True, warmup_ratio=0.06, weight_decay=0.01,
    logging_steps=25, report_to=[])

def acc(p): return {"acc": (np.argmax(p.predictions, -1) == p.label_ids).mean()}
Trainer(model=model, args=args, train_dataset=train, eval_dataset=val,
        compute_metrics=acc).train()
# watch eval_loss per epoch: fall, bottom out (~epoch 2-3), then RISE — pathology ②,
# induced deliberately. load_best_model_at_end rescues the right checkpoint anyway.

Then run the two sabotage experiments: learning_rate=5e-4 (pathology ③ live) and train = train.map(lambda x: {"label": np.random.randint(2)}) (shuffled labels → loss pinned at ln 2 ≈ 0.693: pathology ④). Having caused all three failure shapes once, you'll diagnose them on sight forever — that's the point of the exercise.

If you remember one thing

Data 80 / hyperparameters 15 / everything else 5. Start from standard defaults (2e-5, 2–4 epochs, warmup), read the curves — healthy, overfit, LR-spike, or flat-at-ln(C) — and let best-checkpoint selection plus seed-honesty protect you from your own optimism. The recipe is boring on purpose; boring recipes reproduce.

Interview questions

You have 3k labeled examples and a week. Walk through your fine-tuning plan.

Day 1: data audit — label consistency (double-label a sample, measure agreement), dedup, coverage check, carve val/test with proper (possibly temporal) splits. Day 2: baselines — majority class, TF-IDF+LR, frozen-encoder+head (M3.7's ladder) to know what fine-tuning must beat. Day 3: standard recipe (2e-5, 3 epochs, warmup, best-checkpoint) on DistilBERT/DeBERTa-class; read curves. Day 4: one LR sweep if underfit, data fixes if overfit; 3 seeds on the winner. Day 5: sealed test set once; error analysis on mistakes → next data iteration. The plan's shape (data > baselines > tuning) is what's being graded.

Training loss sits at exactly 0.693 for a binary task and won't move. Diagnose.

0.693 = ln 2 — the model outputs a uniform coin: zero learning (M3.2's sanity check). Since even broken hyperparameters usually learn SOMETHING, suspect data plumbing: labels shuffled or misaligned with inputs, reading the wrong column, all-one-class batches from unshuffled sorted data, tokenizer/model mismatch producing garbage inputs, or the head's output dim wrong. Debug: overfit 10 examples on purpose — if the model can't even memorize 10, the pipeline (not the recipe) is broken.

Why "load best checkpoint" instead of training less in the first place?

Because the optimal stopping point isn't knowable in advance — it depends on data size/quality and moves run to run. Training slightly past the optimum and restoring the best-validation checkpoint gets early stopping's benefit without gambling on a guess; the extra epochs cost only compute. It also gives you the diagnostic curve (you SEE the turn, confirming you found the optimum rather than undershooting it).

How do you decide an improvement is real and not noise?

Compare against run-to-run variance: repeat both configurations across ≥3 seeds; if the mean gap is within the seed spread (±1–2 points is typical for small-data fine-tunes), it's not evidence. Keep the test set out of ALL decisions (val-set improvements decay under experimenter overfitting — M3.3). For decisions that matter, bootstrap confidence intervals on the test predictions. The uncomfortable corollary interviewers like: most small "wins" in ML velocity culture are seed noise, and the discipline to say so is the skill.

Your val metrics are great; production performance is bad. List the usual suspects.

(1) Distribution shift: production inputs differ from training data (time drift, new user segments, different lengths/formats) — check coverage. (2) Leakage inflated offline numbers: near-duplicates across splits, preprocessing fit on all data (M3.3's list). (3) Label definition drift: offline labels don't match what users actually consider correct. (4) Serving skew: different tokenization/truncation/preprocessing in production code vs training code (shockingly common). (5) Feedback effects: the model's own outputs change incoming data. Fix order: reproduce production examples offline first — it localizes which suspect it is.
