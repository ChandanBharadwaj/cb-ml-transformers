Fine-tune vs RAG vs Prompt

Module capstone: the decision you'll actually face, again and again — "the model isn't doing what we want; which lever do we pull?" You now understand all three levers mechanically, which turns a religious debate into an engineering decision. One principle sorts 90% of cases, a decision tree handles the rest, and a cost table settles arguments.

The three levers, in one sentence each

Prompting changes the model's context — instructions, examples (in-context learning, M6.3), constraints — per request, instantly revertible, paid on every call. RAG changes the model's available knowledge — retrieved documents in context at inference — fresh, sourced, updateable by re-indexing. Fine-tuning changes the model's weights — behavior and style permanently, amortized across all future calls (SFT for format, DPO for taste, LoRA for affordability — the last three pages).

The sorting principle

Knowledge goes in the context. Behavior goes in the weights. If the failure is "the model doesn't KNOW something" (your docs, current prices, user data) → RAG; fine-tuning cannot reliably add facts (M8.3's scale-mismatch) and stales instantly. If the failure is "the model doesn't ACT right" (format, tone, domain conventions, consistency) → prompt first, fine-tune when prompting plateaus. Most confusion dissolves under this one test.

Read the columns as a progression, not rivals: prompt → RAG → fine-tune is an escalation ladder, each step justified by the previous one's measured plateau. And they compose — the strongest production systems run all three at once: a fine-tuned model (consistent behavior, domain fluency) fed by RAG (fresh knowledge, citations) under a tight system prompt (guardrails, task frame). Real numbers: one scenario, three price tags

Support bot, 100k queries/month, need: company knowledge + brand tone. Prompt-only (stuff a 3k-token policy digest into every call): ~$113/mo in extra input tokens, stale the day policies change, no sources. RAG: ~$30 one-time indexing + ~$50/mo for 1.5k retrieved tokens/call — current, cited, and cheaper than prompt-stuffing. Tone fine-tune: ~$200 one-time (QLoRA, M8.5) + saves ~800 system-prompt tokens/call ≈ −$30/mo forever. Optimal build: RAG + tone-LoRA + lean prompt ≈ $220 upfront, $20/mo net — cheaper AND better than any single lever. The composition, not the choice, is the answer.

Interview questions

"Should we fine-tune or use RAG?" — give the senior-engineer answer.

Reframe first: they solve different failures. RAG injects KNOWLEDGE at inference — fresh, updateable, attributable; fine-tuning shapes BEHAVIOR in weights — formats, tone, domain conventions, consistency. Fine-tuning is poor at adding facts (tiny gradient pressure vs pretraining scale, instant staleness); RAG can't fix style or reliability. So: sort the actual failures, prompt first for behavior, RAG for knowledge, fine-tune when prompting plateaus at volume — and note they compose (fine-tuned model + RAG + lean prompt is the standard strong build).

When does fine-tuning beat prompting for behavior, concretely?

When (a) prompt-achieved consistency plateaus below requirements (e.g., 95% JSON compliance needed at 99.9%), (b) volume amortizes: per-call token savings (dropped instructions/few-shots) × calls/month exceeds training cost within weeks, (c) prompt real estate is needed for actual content (context window budget), or (d) behavior is too subtle for instructions (brand voice learned from 500 examples beats any adjective list). Prerequisites: stable task definition and enough quality examples — else you're baking in churn (M8.2's data-first rule).

Why can't fine-tuning keep a model's knowledge current?

Three compounding reasons: scale (thousands of examples vs trillions of pretraining tokens — facts get weak gradient support and recall unreliably), staleness (weights freeze at training time; every update is a new training run + eval + deploy cycle, days at best), and forgetting risk (aggressive fact-cramming degrades general behavior — M8.1). RAG's architecture matches knowledge's nature: it changes at data speed, needs attribution, and belongs in a store you can re-index in minutes.

A team fine-tuned an LLM on their wiki to "teach it the company" and results disappoint. Post-mortem?

Predictable failure: raw wiki text isn't (instruction, response) pairs, so SFT taught wiki-STYLE continuation, not question-answering over wiki FACTS; fact recall from light fine-tuning is spotty (scale mismatch); knowledge froze at training day; and no attribution exists for verification. Correct build: RAG over the wiki (chunk, embed, retrieve — their M12.1 pipeline), optionally + a small SFT for answer format/tone. Salvage: keep their eval set — it becomes the RAG golden set (M12.3). This exact scenario is a top-3 applied-LLM interview question because half the industry lived it.

How do the three levers interact with model-tier choice?

Tier sets the capability ceiling the levers work under: no lever adds reasoning a model lacks (the tree's third branch). Interactions: stronger models need less few-shot scaffolding (prompt savings) and follow instructions better (delaying fine-tuning's justification); weaker/cheaper models + LoRA can match stronger models on NARROW tasks at far lower serving cost (M7.4's overtrained-small-model economics applied to your stack) — the classic cost play: frontier model to prototype and generate training data, tuned small model to serve. State it as a 2D decision — tier × lever — and you sound like you've shipped.
