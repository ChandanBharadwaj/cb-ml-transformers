Scaling Laws & Chinchilla

The strangest empirical fact in modern AI: model quality improves with scale in a way you can draw a ruler through. Loss versus parameters, data, or compute — on log-log axes, straight lines, holding across seven orders of magnitude. This page covers the lines, the famous correction (Chinchilla) that embarrassed a generation of models, why today's models deliberately "overtrain" past that optimum, and the debate about capabilities that seem to appear suddenly.

The lines: loss is predictable

Chinchilla: the ratio everyone had wrong

Given a fixed compute budget, you choose: bigger model on less data, or smaller model on more data? (Compute ≈ 6 × params × tokens — the constraint line.) Kaplan-era wisdom said favor parameters, so GPT-3 went 175B params on just 300B tokens. DeepMind's Chinchilla paper (2022) redid the measurement carefully and found the optimum at roughly ~20 tokens per parameter — meaning GPT-3 should have been ¼ the size on 4× the data. Their proof: Chinchilla, 70B on 1.4T tokens, same compute as the 280B Gopher — and better at everything. An entire generation of models turned out to be oversized and undertrained.

Why modern models "overtrain" past Chinchilla

Llama-3-8B trained on 15T tokens — 1,875 tokens per parameter, ~90× the Chinchilla ratio. Waste? No: Chinchilla optimizes training compute only. But a model is trained once and served billions of times — and serving cost scales with parameters (M7.2, M7.5). Overtraining a small model spends extra training compute to buy a cheaper-forever inference artifact: an 8B that punches above its weight beats a Chinchilla-optimal 30B that costs 4× per query. The optimum moved because the objective did — from "best model per training dollar" to "best model per lifetime dollar." That's also the honest economics behind the small-but-great model wave (Phi, Qwen-small, Llama-8B) you deploy from.

Emergence: real jumps, or measurement artifacts?

While loss scales smoothly, specific capabilities often appear suddenly: arithmetic, in-context learning (M6.3), instruction-following — near-zero below some scale, then rapidly present. Two readings, both worth knowing: Emergence is real — some skills need enough sub-components in place before working at all (a lock needs every pin). Emergence is partly an artifact — a 2023 Stanford analysis showed many "jumps" reflect all-or-nothing metrics (exact-match arithmetic scores 0 until every digit is right) while underlying per-token likelihoods improve smoothly; smoother metrics smooth the curves. Practical stance: capabilities do arrive with scale and are hard to predict individually — but treat dramatic "phase transition" claims with M3.6's metric-skepticism.

Real numbers

The ratio across eras: GPT-3 175B ÷ 300B tokens ≈ 1.7 tok/param (starved) · Chinchilla 70B ÷ 1.4T = 20 (optimal) · Llama-3-8B ÷ 15T = 1,875 (deliberately overtrained for cheap serving). Budget math you can now do: at ~6·N·D FLOPs, Llama-3-8B's pretraining ≈ 6 × 8e9 × 15e12 ≈ 7×10²³ FLOPs; on H100s at ~40% utilization that's ~1.3M GPU-hours — a few thousand GPUs for a few weeks, matching Meta's disclosures. Scaling laws turned model planning into spreadsheet engineering.

Connect the dots

This page explains model-picker economics you navigate weekly: why providers offer small/medium/frontier tiers (points along the lines at different lifetime-cost targets), why small models keep getting shockingly good (the overtraining play), and why M6.3's GPT-2 failed your arithmetic prompt while modern models pass it (capability-arrival with scale). When a vendor says "our 8B matches last year's 70B," you now know the two levers that make that sentence possible: more tokens per parameter, better tokens.

Build: fit a scaling law with your own ruler
import numpy as np

# published-ish (compute FLOPs, loss) points — the kind labs fit
runs = [(1e19, 3.40), (1e20, 3.07), (1e21, 2.77), (1e22, 2.50), (1e23, 2.26)]

logC = np.log10([r[0] for r in runs])
logL = np.log10([r[1] for r in runs])
alpha, b = np.polyfit(logC, logL, 1)           # straight line in log-log
print(f"loss ≈ {10**b:.2f} · C^{alpha:.3f}")

# extrapolate a 10× bigger run BEFORE paying for it:
C_next = 1e24
pred = 10**b * C_next**alpha
print(f"predicted loss at 1e24 FLOPs: {pred:.2f}")
print(f"perplexity (M4.1!): {np.e**pred:.1f}")   # loss → hesitation-width

Five points, one polyfit — you just did what frontier labs do with their pilot runs. Change one input loss by 0.1 and watch the extrapolation move: the sensitivity you feel is why labs run many small runs before believing the ruler with nine figures of budget.

If you remember one thing

Loss follows power laws in params, data, and compute — predictable enough to budget $100M runs from small pilots. Chinchilla: at fixed training compute, ~20 tokens/param is optimal — but modern models overtrain far past it because inference, not training, dominates lifetime cost. Capabilities arrive with scale — sometimes genuinely suddenly, sometimes just measured suddenly. Scale is a strategy because the ruler keeps working.

Interview questions

What are neural scaling laws and why did they reorganize the field?

Empirical power-law relationships: LM loss decreases predictably as a function of parameters, dataset size, and compute (straight lines on log-log axes, holding over many orders of magnitude). Consequences: capability became purchasable and predictable — labs could extrapolate a giant run's loss from cheap pilots, justifying massive investments (GPT-3 was the bet that the line continues); research priorities shifted from architecture novelty toward scale, data and efficiency. They're empirical regularities, not derived theory — but they've held remarkably.

State the Chinchilla result and its practical rule.

For a fixed training-compute budget (≈6·N·D FLOPs), loss is minimized by scaling parameters N and tokens D together, at roughly D ≈ 20N — not by maximizing N as earlier work suggested. Demonstration: Chinchilla-70B (1.4T tokens) beat Gopher-280B (300B tokens) at equal compute. Rule: a compute-optimal 10B model wants ~200B training tokens. It reframed GPT-3-era models as undertrained.

Why do current models train far beyond the Chinchilla-optimal ratio?

Chinchilla optimizes training cost alone; production optimizes lifetime cost = training + (inference × billions of queries), and inference scales with parameter count. Overtraining a smaller model (Llama-3-8B: ~1,875 tok/param) sacrifices training-compute efficiency to produce an artifact that's cheaper at every future query, easier to deploy (fits smaller GPUs, M7.2), and faster (latency). The optimum shifted because the objective did — an excellent example of ML economics overriding ML theory.

Is "emergence" of capabilities real? Give the two-sided answer.

Observed: certain capabilities (multi-step arithmetic, ICL, instruction-following) go from ~absent to present over a modest scale range while loss improves smoothly. Skeptical side (Schaeffer et al. 2023): many sharp jumps are artifacts of discontinuous metrics — exact-match scoring hides smooth per-token improvement; with continuous metrics, curves flatten. Synthesis: underlying competence accrues smoothly, but thresholded task success (which is what users experience) can genuinely switch on abruptly — so emergence is real at the product level even where it's smooth at the loss level. Both halves stated = strong answer.

Estimate training FLOPs for a 8B model on 15T tokens, and rough GPU time.

Rule: ≈6 × N × D = 6 × 8e9 × 1.5e13 ≈ 7.2×10²³ FLOPs. An H100 delivers ~1e15 FLOP/s at ~40% utilization → ~4e14 effective; 7.2e23 / 4e14 ≈ 1.8e9 GPU-seconds ≈ 500k–1.5M GPU-hours depending on utilization — i.e., a few thousand GPUs for several weeks. Knowing the 6ND rule and utilization reality (30–50%) lets you sanity-check any training-cost claim in a meeting, which is exactly why interviewers ask it.
