GPT & Causal Language Modeling

The other branch of the 2018 fork. Where BERT bet on bidirectional understanding, the GPT line bet on M5.7's masked decoder and the simplest objective in the book — predict the next token — then discovered that scaling that one bet produces something qualitatively new: models you can teach with examples in the prompt, no gradient updates at all. That discovery, in-context learning, is why the LLM era looks the way it does — and why you prompt models instead of training them.

Causal LM: every position is a training example

The objective needs one paragraph because M5.7 built it: causal mask on, every position predicts its successor, all positions in parallel, cross-entropy averaged. What deserves emphasis is the density: a 1,000-token document yields 1,000 predictions per pass (MLM: only the masked 15%, i.e. 150). More signal per pass, from any raw text, no masking decisions — the operational simplicity that later made trillion-token training runs tractable.

The scaling story: 117M → 1.5B → 175B

In-context learning: the capability that changed everything

The GPT-3 paper's bombshell: show the model a few input→output examples inside the prompt, and it continues the pattern — task "learned" with zero weight updates. Translation from three examples. Format conversion from two. Classification from five. The model, having spent pretraining compressing every pattern the internet contains, learned the meta-skill of recognizing and continuing patterns — and a prompt's examples are just one more pattern to continue.

Vocabulary you'll use in interviews: zero-shot (instruction only), few-shot (instruction + examples), and the mechanism behind both is this page. One honest caveat: in-context "learning" is pattern induction over the context window — it doesn't persist, doesn't update knowledge, and can be brittle to example choice and order. Powerful, not magic.

Real numbers

GPT-3's few-shot translation: with 32 in-prompt examples it approached fine-tuned systems of its day. The dials from GPT-2 to GPT-3, in config terms you now read fluently (M5.6): d 1600→12288, L 48→96, heads 25→96, context 1024→2048, params 1.5B→175B, data ~40GB→~570GB. Compute: ~3,640 petaFLOP-days ≈ $4.6M of 2020 cloud GPU time — the number that made "scaling laws" a boardroom phrase.

Connect the dots

Every prompt-side trick you use daily is applied in-context learning: few-shot examples in your chatbot's system prompt; the retrieved chunks in your RAG pipeline (documents as "examples" the model reads at inference — knowledge in-context instead of in-weights); even tool-calling schemas (M12.4) are patterns the model continues. And one clarification this page arms you with: the base GPT here completes text — it doesn't chat, follow instructions, or refuse anything. The gap between this page's model and your chatbot's behavior is entirely M8.3–8.4 (SFT and RLHF). Base ≠ assistant.

Build: watch in-context learning turn on
from transformers import pipeline
gen = pipeline("text-generation", model="gpt2", max_new_tokens=8)

zero_shot = "Translate English to French:\nplush giraffe →"
few_shot  = ("Translate English to French:\n"
             "sea otter → loutre de mer\n"
             "cheese → fromage\n"
             "mint → menthe\n"
             "plush giraffe →")

print("ZERO-SHOT:", gen(zero_shot)[0]["generated_text"][len(zero_shot):])
print("FEW-SHOT :", gen(few_shot)[0]["generated_text"][len(few_shot):])

GPT-2 (1.5B, pre-ICL-era) usually fumbles both — try the same pair against any modern model API and few-shot snaps into place. You're measuring the capability that emerged between 2019 and 2020, with your own prompts. Also instructive: give GPT-2 a bare question and watch it continue rather than answer — the base-model behavior that M8.3 exists to fix.

If you remember one thing

The GPT line = M5.7's masked decoder + next-token prediction + relentless scaling. Its gift was in-context learning: at sufficient scale, examples in the prompt induce task behavior with no weight updates — the discovery that made prompting a programming model and set up everything in the LLM era. BERT understands deeply; GPT generates and adapts on the fly; you now know exactly why each is shaped for its job.

Interview questions

Compare MLM and causal LM as pretraining objectives.

MLM (BERT): predict 15% hidden tokens from bidirectional context — richer per-token representations, ideal for understanding tasks; but only 15% of positions give signal per pass, and no natural generation. Causal LM (GPT): predict every next token left-to-right — 100% of positions give signal, arbitrarily scalable on raw text, natively generative; but each prediction sees only left context. The field's split: encoders/MLM for representation, decoders/causal for generation — with causal winning the scale race on simplicity and signal density.

What is in-context learning, mechanically?

Behavior where a pretrained LM performs a task after seeing input→output examples in its prompt, with no parameter updates: the model treats the examples as a pattern and generates the most probable continuation. It emerges at scale (barely present in GPT-2, strong in GPT-3+); research framings include implicit Bayesian inference and induction-head circuits that copy-and-extend patterns. Practical limits: bounded by the context window, sensitive to example choice/order/format, and nothing persists across calls.

Why did next-token prediction scale better than masked LM?

Signal density (every token supervises, vs 15%), zero preprocessing decisions (no mask rates or corruption schemes — just raw text), a single objective that doubles as the deployment interface (training task = generation task, no head-swapping), and natural compatibility with the KV-cache serving pattern. Each factor is small; multiplied over trillion-token runs and production serving they decided the era. (M6.4 adds T5's evidence to this story.)

A base (non-instruct) model answers your question with more questions. Why?

Because it's a text-continuation engine, not an assistant: given "What is the capital of France?" the statistically likely continuations include more quiz questions, exam formatting, or forum context — whatever followed such text in the corpus. Nothing in pretraining rewards answering. Instruction tuning (SFT, M8.3) and preference tuning (RLHF, M8.4) reshape the distribution toward helpful responses. Distinguishing base-model behavior from assistant behavior is a reliable interview discriminator.

When do few-shot examples beat fine-tuning, and vice versa?

Few-shot wins when: examples are scarce (<100), tasks change often, you can't host/modify weights, or latency of iteration matters (edit a prompt vs run a training job). Fine-tuning wins when: you have thousands of consistent examples, need maximum quality/consistency, want shorter prompts (cost — examples are re-billed every call!), or need behavior no prompt reliably elicits. Production pattern: prototype few-shot, graduate to fine-tuning when volume and stability justify it (the M8.6 decision framework).
