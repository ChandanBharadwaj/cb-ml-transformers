Pretraining: The Big Idea

You now own the machine. Modules 6–8 are about how it gets filled — and the filling happens in a very particular economic shape: someone spends millions teaching a model language once, and everyone else adapts that model cheaply, forever. That split — pretrain once, adapt many times — is the single most important structural fact about the modern AI industry, and it decides what you, personally, will ever train versus download.

Self-supervision at continental scale

The trick is one you've now seen twice — word2vec's neighbor game (M2.2), the decoder's next-token game (M5.7): raw text labels itself. No annotation budget exists that could label trillions of examples, but none is needed — hide part of the data, predict it from the rest, and every sentence on the internet becomes supervised training signal. Pretraining is exactly this, run on everything crawlable, until the loss stops falling.

What the corpus actually is (and its consequences)

"Trained on the internet" hides real engineering. The pipeline: crawl (CommonCrawl and friends) → filter for quality (classifiers score "is this coherent text?") → deduplicate aggressively (repeated text causes memorization — 3.3's overfitting at corpus scale) → mix deliberately (X% web, Y% code, Z% books; code fraction turns out to matter even for reasoning) → tokenize (5.1) → train. Every property of the corpus becomes a property of the model: its knowledge cutoff (the crawl date), its languages (the mix), its biases (M2.4's lesson, at scale), and — a problem you'll meet in M11.4 — its accidental memorization of benchmark test sets (contamination).

Why nobody trains from scratch (including your employer)

The arithmetic is brutal and worth having ready: competence at language requires seeing more text than any single company's data could provide, and compute measured in GPU-years. Meanwhile a pretrained base already contains that competence, and adapting it to your task needs 100–10,000 examples (M8.1 explains why so few suffice: the body already knows language; you're only steering it). Training from scratch to save licensing costs is like refining your own gasoline to save on fuel. The rational stack — the one you already run — is: someone else's pretrained model + your data + cheap adaptation.

Real numbers

The scale-up in one line each: BERT (2018) pretrained on 3.3B words; GPT-3 (2020) ~300B tokens; Llama-3 (2024) 15T tokens — a ~4,500× data jump in six years. Compute: BERT ≈ days on 64 TPU cores; Llama-3-70B ≈ weeks on ~16,000 H100s (M7.3 prices this properly). Meanwhile the adaptation you'll do in M9.5 — fine-tuning MiniLM on your RAG pairs — runs on one modest GPU in under an hour. Same industry, twelve orders of magnitude apart, connected by a download link.

If you remember one thing

Pretraining = self-supervised prediction over a curated slice of the internet, paid for once at eye-watering cost, producing a base model everyone else adapts for pocket change. The corpus's properties become the model's properties — knowledge, cutoff, biases, contamination — and your entire professional relationship to these models lives on the cheap side of the pretrain/adapt divide.

Interview questions

What is pretraining, and why is self-supervision essential to it?

Pretraining optimizes a general objective (next-token or masked-token prediction) over a massive unlabeled corpus, producing broad language competence before any task-specific training. Self-supervision is what makes the scale possible: the text provides its own labels (the hidden token), so supervision scales with data collection rather than annotation budgets — trillions of examples for free.

Why does corpus deduplication matter so much for LLMs?

Repeated documents make the model memorize rather than generalize (overfitting at corpus scale), waste compute on redundant gradient signal, amplify whatever the duplicated text says, and worsen verbatim regurgitation risks (privacy/copyright). Empirically, deduplicated data gives better loss per token trained — one of the cheapest quality wins in the pipeline, which is why every modern recipe includes aggressive fuzzy dedup.

What determines a model's "knowledge cutoff", and what are the workarounds?

The crawl/collection date of its pretraining corpus — the model has simply never seen later text. Workarounds move knowledge to inference time: RAG (retrieve current documents into context — your system), tool use (search APIs, M12.4), or continual fine-tuning (expensive, risks forgetting). Knowing that "the model doesn't know X" is a data property, not an intelligence property, is the practical takeaway.

Your CTO asks whether to pretrain a company LLM from scratch. Give the engineer's answer.

Almost certainly no: competitive base models cost $10M+ in compute alone, need trillions of tokens (more than any single company's data), and months of specialized infrastructure work — while fine-tuning or RAG over an open base (Llama/Qwen-class) captures nearly all domain value for 3–5 orders of magnitude less. The exceptions are real but narrow: extreme data sensitivity with regulatory bars on external weights, truly non-natural-language domains (protein sequences), or being an AI lab. Recommend: budget toward data quality, adaptation (M8), and evaluation instead.

How does the pretraining data mix affect downstream behavior?

Directly: language proportions set multilingual competence (and tokenizer efficiency, M5.1); code fraction improves not just coding but measurably reasoning/structure; books vs web shifts style and long-form coherence; the crawl's demographics import its biases (M2.4 at scale); and any benchmark text that leaks in inflates evals (contamination, M11.4). Data mixing is now a first-class research area — "what did it eat?" explains more model behavior than most architecture details.
