BERT & Masked Language Modeling

2018, the encoder's big moment. BERT took your M5 encoder stack and asked: what pretraining game fits a model that sees both directions? Next-token prediction is out — a bidirectional model would just read the answer (5.7's cheating problem). The solution, masked language modeling, is a cloze test at internet scale — and it produced the ancestor of both models you use daily: MiniLM and GLiNER's backbone.

MLM: the fill-in-the-blank game

Take a sentence, hide 15% of its tokens, ask the model to reconstruct them — using context from both sides. Loss = cross-entropy on the hidden positions only. That's masked language modeling. The bidirectionality that disqualified BERT from generation becomes its superpower here: every blank is solved with full-sentence evidence, forcing representations that integrate left and right context — exactly what understanding tasks need.

The 80/10/10 trick (a real interview favorite)

Subtlety: if masked positions were always shown as [MASK], the model would learn "only compute hard when I see the [MASK] token" — but at fine-tuning and inference time, [MASK] never appears, so those habits would misfire. BERT's fix: of the 15% selected positions, 80% become [MASK], 10% are swapped for a random word, and 10% are left unchanged — but all 15% must be predicted. Consequence: the model can never trust any token fully and must maintain rich contextual representations for every position. A tiny recipe detail that materially decides representation quality.

Anatomy: [CLS], [SEP], and the NSP detour

BERT's inputs carry two special tokens you've seen in every tokenizer dump: [CLS] prepended (a workspace position whose final vector serves as a whole-sequence summary — the classification handle) and [SEP] marking boundaries between paired inputs (question/passage, sentence A/B). Original BERT also trained a second objective — next sentence prediction (did sentence B really follow A?) — which RoBERTa (2019) showed was unnecessary: dropping NSP and just training MLM longer on more data worked better. Worth knowing as the canonical example that pretraining recipes, not just architectures, were still being debugged years after the transformer.

Family tree in three one-liners: RoBERTa = BERT minus NSP, plus 10× data and tuning — same architecture, better recipe. DistilBERT = BERT distilled to 6 layers, 40% smaller, ~97% of the quality (M9.4's technique). ALBERT = parameter-sharing across layers — clever, memory-light, now mostly historical. All are "BERT family": encoder + MLM.

Real numbers

Real fill-mask output from bert-base-uncased for "The doctor [MASK] the patient's leg": examined .19 · treated .11 · amputated .09 · bandaged .07 · broke .05 — medical verbs dominating, both context sides audibly at work. Training: 3.3B words, 1M steps, batch 256, ~4 days on 64 TPU chips (2018 dollars: ~$7k of compute — quaint against Llama-3's ~$100M six years later). The 15% mask rate: chosen empirically; later work (like ELECTRA's all-positions objective) squeezed more signal per sentence.

Connect the dots

This page is your MiniLM's birth certificate, part 1: all-MiniLM-L6-v2's backbone was pretrained exactly this way — WordPiece tokens, [CLS]/[SEP], MLM over billions of words — before M9's distillation and contrastive tuning made it an embedder. GLiNER's encoder: same story (M10). And a practical echo you've already coded around: sentence-transformers' outputs include those special tokens' positions — mean-pooling (M9.1) deliberately handles them. When you see [CLS] in a tokenizer dump now, you know its job.

Build: play the MLM game against real BERT
from transformers import pipeline

fill = pipeline("fill-mask", model="bert-base-uncased")

for s in ["The doctor [MASK] the patient's leg.",
          "The capital of Karnataka is [MASK].",
          "I deposited money at the [MASK].",
          "We sat on the river [MASK]."]:
    print("\n" + s)
    for r in fill(s, top_k=4):
        print(f"   {r['token_str']:>12}  {r['score']:.3f}")

The last two prompts are M2.4's bank problem, interrogated directly: watch the distribution shift between money-context and river-context. You're watching bidirectional attention (M5.3's machinery) resolve the ambiguity that static embeddings structurally couldn't — the entire course arc in four API calls.

If you remember one thing

BERT = the M5 encoder + masked language modeling: hide 15% (with the 80/10/10 twist), predict from both sides, loss on masked positions only. Bidirectionality trades generation away for the richest per-token understanding — which is why the BERT family became the embedding/classification/NER workhorse, and why your MiniLM and GLiNER are its descendants.

Interview questions

Why can't BERT use next-token prediction, and why can't GPT use MLM's bidirectionality?

BERT's attention is bidirectional — for next-token prediction every position could see its answer (5.7's cheating problem), so the objective would collapse. GPT's causal mask exists precisely to make next-token prediction honest, but it thereby denies right-context — it can't fill a blank using what follows. Objective and attention pattern must be chosen together: MLM ↔ bidirectional encoder, causal LM ↔ masked decoder.

Explain the 80/10/10 masking strategy and its purpose.

Of the 15% of positions selected for prediction: 80% are replaced with [MASK], 10% with a random token, 10% left unchanged — all still predicted. Purpose: [MASK] never appears downstream, so a model that only "works hard" at [MASK] positions would carry a train/test mismatch; random and unchanged variants force it to maintain full contextual representations everywhere and to distrust surface tokens. It's the classic "small detail, big representational consequence" question.

What is the [CLS] token and is it automatically a good sentence embedding?

A special position prepended to every input whose final hidden state is trained (via fine-tuning heads, originally NSP) to summarize the sequence — the standard hook for classification. But out-of-the-box it is NOT a good similarity embedding: without similarity-oriented training its geometry is poor (raw-BERT cosine scores are notoriously degenerate — the anisotropy problem, M9.1). Sentence-transformers exist precisely to fix this with pooling + contrastive fine-tuning.

What did RoBERTa change and what's the lesson?

Same architecture as BERT; recipe changes only: drop NSP, train much longer on ~10× data, bigger batches, dynamic masking (re-mask each epoch), byte-level BPE. Result: substantially better across the board. Lesson: pretraining hyperparameters and data scale can matter as much as architecture — "BERT was undertrained" — and claims of architectural superiority need recipe-controlled comparisons. A favorite question for probing whether candidates separate architecture from training effects.

When would you reach for a BERT-family model in 2025+ instead of an LLM?

Whenever the output is a vector or a label rather than prose, at volume: embeddings/retrieval (MiniLM-class models embed thousands of texts/sec on CPU), classification, NER (M10), reranking (M12.2) — at 1/100th to 1/1000th the serving cost of a decoder LLM, with bidirectional context that's genuinely better per token for understanding. The mature answer: LLMs for generation and complex reasoning; encoders for the high-QPS representation layer underneath — most production stacks (including the candidate's own RAG) run both.
