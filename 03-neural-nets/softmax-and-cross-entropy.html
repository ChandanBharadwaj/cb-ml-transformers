Softmax & Cross-Entropy

This page explains two formulas that run your daily life. Softmax converts raw scores into probabilities — it fires every time an LLM picks its next token, and the temperature parameter in your API calls is a dial inside it. Cross-entropy is the loss those models trained on — the single number that GPT-4 spent its $100M compute budget pushing down. After this page, neither is folklore.

Softmax: scores → probabilities

A model's last layer outputs one logit (raw score, last page's word) per option — per class, or per token in the vocabulary. Logits can be any numbers: 3.2, −1.7, 0.4. To act on them we need probabilities: non-negative, summing to 1. Softmax does it in two moves: exponentiate each logit (making everything positive and amplifying gaps), then normalize by the sum.

P(i) = ezᵢ / Σⱼ ezⱼ
In plain words: raise e to each score, divide each result by the total. Bigger score → disproportionately bigger share. The Σ in the denominator is the "compare against everyone" that negative sampling (M2.3) dodged during training — here, at inference over classes or one vocabulary, it's affordable.

Why exponentials, specifically? Three properties earn softmax its job: it makes everything positive; it's winner-amplifying (a logit gap of 3 becomes a probability ratio of e³ ≈ 20×, so confident scores translate to confident probabilities); and it's smooth — differentiable everywhere, so gradients flow through it (which a hard argmax "pick the max" could never allow; you can't take a slope of a cliff). It's the differentiable version of choosing.

Cross-entropy: the loss that trained everything you use

Softmax gives the model's belief. Training needs a wrongness score (M1.4's loss) comparing belief to truth. Cross-entropy is brutally simple: look up the probability the model gave to the correct answer, and take −log of it. Everything else is ignored.

loss = −log P(correct answer)
In plain words: a surprise meter. Gave the truth P=0.95? loss = 0.05 — barely surprised. P=0.5? loss = 0.69. P=0.01? loss = 4.6 — shocked, huge gradient, big weight updates. As P→0 the loss →∞: total confidence in the wrong thing is punished without limit.

Why not just use accuracy as the loss?

Accuracy is what you care about, so why train on cross-entropy? Because accuracy is a staircase: nudge a weight slightly and accuracy either doesn't move or jumps a whole step — its slope is 0 almost everywhere, and M1.4's ball can't roll on a staircase. Cross-entropy is a smooth ramp measuring how confidently right or wrong you are, so every weight nudge changes it a little, and gradients always have a direction to follow. General principle worth memorizing: train on smooth surrogates, evaluate on the metric you care about (that metric page is 3.6).

Real numbers

LLM training loss is cross-entropy over the vocabulary, averaged per token — when papers report "loss 2.0", that means the model gives the true next token e−2.0 ≈ 13.5% probability on average (equivalently: perplexity ≈ 7.4, "hesitating between ~7 words" — formalized in M4.1). Llama-3's pretraining ended around loss ≈ 1.9 on its data mix; random guessing over a 128k vocabulary would be loss = ln(128,000) ≈ 11.8. The entire $100M+ run: moving one number from ~11.8 to ~1.9.

Connect the dots

Three sightings in your own stack. (1) temperature=0.7 in your chatbot config: dividing logits inside this page's softmax, nothing more. (2) The σ in M2.3's negative sampling: softmax's 2-option special case (sigmoid). (3) "logprobs" in API responses: the log of these very probabilities. And one forward pointer: when your LLM streams a response, every single token was chosen by running this page's math over ~100k vocabulary entries — softmax at the scale negative sampling avoided, paid at inference time instead (why that's affordable: M7.5).

Build: softmax, temperature and CE in 12 lines
import numpy as np

def softmax(z, T=1.0):
    z = np.array(z, dtype=float) / T
    z -= z.max()                  # numerical-stability trick (standard practice)
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

logits = [7.1, 3.9, 2.8, -1.0]    # Paris, Lyon, nice, banana
for T in [0.3, 1.0, 2.0]:
    print(f"T={T}:", softmax(logits, T).round(3))

truth = 0                          # Paris is correct
for T in [0.3, 1.0, 2.0]:
    p = softmax(logits, T)[truth]
    print(f"T={T}: P(truth)={p:.3f}  CE loss={-np.log(p):.3f}")

Reproduce the animation exactly, then break things: set a logit to 50 and see why the z -= z.max() line exists (remove it → overflow → NaN — the real-world bug it prevents). You've now implemented the two formulas that trained and steer every model you use.

If you remember one thing

Softmax = exponentiate-and-normalize, turning logits into probabilities; temperature is a divisor inside it that sharpens (T<1) or flattens (T>1) the result. Cross-entropy = −log P(truth): a surprise meter whose steep left wall makes confidently-wrong predictions the loudest teachers. Every modern language model is these two formulas plus a very large amount of M1.

Interview questions

Walk through what softmax does and why exponentiation is used.

Softmax exponentiates each logit and normalizes by the sum, producing a valid probability distribution. Exponentiation: guarantees positivity, amplifies differences (logit gap Δ → probability ratio e^Δ, so clear winners get decisive mass), and keeps the whole map smooth/differentiable so it can sit inside gradient-based training — unlike a hard argmax.

Explain temperature mathematically and behaviorally.

softmax(z/T). T<1 scales logits up → sharper distribution → conservative, repetitive, deterministic-leaning output (T→0 = argmax). T>1 scales logits down → flatter distribution → diverse, creative, riskier output. It reshapes existing beliefs; it never adds knowledge. Practical defaults: ~0 for extraction/structured output, ~0.7 for chat, 1.0+ for brainstorming.

Why is cross-entropy preferred over accuracy or squared error for classification?

Accuracy has zero gradient almost everywhere (a step function of the weights) — unusable for gradient descent. Squared error on probabilities is smooth but its gradients shrink exactly when the model is confidently wrong (paired with sigmoid/softmax it saturates), learning slowest when it should learn fastest. Cross-entropy's −log P blows up for confident errors, delivering large gradients precisely there; with softmax it also yields the elegant gradient (p − y), simple and well-scaled.

Your training loss is exactly ln(number of classes) at step 0. What does that tell you?

The model is outputting a uniform distribution — P(truth) = 1/C so loss = ln C. That's the expected starting loss with random init, and it's a standard sanity check: for 10 classes expect ~2.30, for a 50k vocab ~10.8. Starting far above it suggests broken initialization or preprocessing; never dropping below it means nothing is being learned (e.g., shuffled labels, LR too high).

Connect LLM "loss" numbers to something interpretable.

LLM loss is mean per-token cross-entropy: loss L means the true next token gets e^(−L) average probability, and perplexity = e^L is "the effective number of tokens the model hesitates between." Loss 2.0 → ~13.5% on the truth, perplexity ~7.4. This converts leaderboard numbers into intuition and is exactly the M4.1 material interviewers probe with "what does a lower loss actually mean?"

Does temperature=0 make an LLM deterministic and correct?

Deterministic-ish (greedy argmax per step; some nondeterminism can remain from floating-point/batching effects in serving) — but not more correct: it just always picks the modal token. If the model's beliefs are wrong, T=0 confidently commits to the same wrong answer every time. Temperature trades variance, not truthfulness — a distinction interviewers use to separate users from engineers.
