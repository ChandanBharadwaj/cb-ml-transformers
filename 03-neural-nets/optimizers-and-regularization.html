Optimizers & Regularization

Raw gradient descent works, but nobody ships it. Every real config you'll ever read says AdamW, has a warmup, a weight_decay, and somewhere a dropout. This page explains all four so that training configs stop containing magic words — and gives you the two-line answers interviews expect for each.

From SGD to Adam: smarter downhill

SGD is M1.4 verbatim: step against the current batch's gradient. Its weakness: in narrow valleys, batch noise makes it zig-zag across the walls instead of walking along the floor. Momentum fixes this with physics — keep a running average of recent gradients (a "velocity") and step along that. Zig-zag components cancel across batches; the consistent downhill component accumulates. A heavy ball instead of a drunk hiker.

Adam adds a second trick: a per-parameter step size. It tracks each parameter's typical gradient magnitude and divides by it — parameters with tiny, rare gradients (an embedding row for a rare word) get boosted; ones with huge gradients get damped. Momentum + per-parameter scaling = fast, forgiving, the default everywhere. AdamW is Adam with weight decay done correctly (decoupled from the gradient math) — when in doubt, the answer is AdamW.

Learning-rate schedules: warmup, then decay

The learning rate isn't one number — it's a schedule. The near-universal shape for transformers: warmup (start near zero, ramp up over the first few hundred/thousand steps) then decay (linear or cosine down to ~zero). Why warmup? At initialization, weights are random and Adam's per-parameter statistics are garbage; full-size steps in that state can shove the model into a bad region it never escapes. Warmup lets the statistics stabilize before real steps begin. Why decay? Early, big steps to cross the landscape; late, small steps to settle precisely into the valley floor — M1.4's "bouncing" animation, prevented by design.

Regularization: the anti-memorization toolkit

3.3's diverging curves showed the disease (overfitting); here are the two standard vaccines you'll actually configure:

Weight decay — every step, shrink all weights slightly toward zero (weight_decay=0.01 in AdamW). Effect: the model can only keep weights large if the data keeps insisting they earn it. Memorization patterns — supported by few examples — can't pay the rent; general patterns can. Occam's razor as a subtraction.

Dropout — during training only, randomly zero a fraction (typically 10% in transformers) of activations each step. No neuron can rely on a specific partner existing, so fragile co-adapted circuits — memorization's favorite trick — can't form; features must be redundantly, robustly encoded. At inference, dropout turns off entirely. (It's also a beautiful ensemble argument: each step trains a random sub-network; inference averages them all.)

Real numbers: BERT's actual recipe

From the BERT paper, now fully readable to you: optimizer Adam (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999 — the momentum and magnitude averaging constants), LR 1e-4 with 10,000-step warmup then linear decay, weight decay 0.01, dropout 0.1 on every layer, batch 256 sequences, 1M steps. MiniLM inherits the same skeleton. When you fine-tune in M8/M9, you'll shrink exactly two of these (LR → 2e-5, steps → a few hundred) and keep the rest.

Connect the dots

Open any Hugging Face example you've ever copy-pasted: optim="adamw_torch", warmup_ratio=0.1, weight_decay=0.01 — three of this page's four ideas, sitting in code you've already run. The fourth (dropout) is inside the model config (hidden_dropout_prob: 0.1 in MiniLM's). Nothing in a training config is folklore anymore: every field is either loop mechanics (3.3), an optimizer knob, a schedule, or a regularizer.

Build: race SGD vs momentum vs Adam yourself
import numpy as np

# the animation's valley: steep in y, gentle in x. Minimum at (0,0).
loss_grad = lambda p: np.array([0.2*p[0], 4.0*p[1]])   # gradient of 0.1x² + 2y²

def run(kind, lr=0.09, steps=60):
    p = np.array([-8.0, 2.5]); v = np.zeros(2); m = np.zeros(2); s = np.zeros(2)
    for t in range(1, steps+1):
        g = loss_grad(p)
        if kind == "sgd":      p -= lr * g
        elif kind == "mom":    v = 0.9*v + g; p -= lr * v
        elif kind == "adam":
            m = 0.9*m + 0.1*g; s = 0.999*s + 0.001*g*g
            mh, sh = m/(1-0.9**t), s/(1-0.999**t)
            p -= lr * mh / (np.sqrt(sh) + 1e-8)
    return p

for k in ["sgd", "mom", "adam"]:
    print(k, "→ final position:", run(k).round(4), "(target: [0, 0])")

Adam lands closest with the same budget. Then sabotage it: raise lr until SGD diverges and note that Adam survives much longer — its per-parameter scaling is a built-in shock absorber. That robustness, more than raw speed, is why it became the default.

If you remember one thing

AdamW + warmup-then-decay + weight decay + dropout is the standard kit: momentum smooths the walk, per-parameter scaling adapts it, warmup protects the fragile start, decay lands the ending, and the two regularizers tax memorization. You now know why each line of a training config exists — which is precisely the difference between running a recipe and cooking.

Interview questions

Explain momentum in optimization — what problem does it solve?

Plain SGD follows each mini-batch's noisy gradient, zig-zagging in ravine-shaped loss surfaces (steep across, gentle along). Momentum maintains an exponential moving average of gradients and steps along it: oscillating components cancel between batches while the consistent descent direction accumulates — faster progress and less sensitivity to noise. Physically: a ball with inertia instead of a walker who re-decides direction every step.

What does Adam add over SGD+momentum, and what is AdamW?

Adam keeps two running averages per parameter: the gradient (momentum, β₁) and the squared gradient (magnitude, β₂), then steps by momentum ÷ √magnitude — an individual, self-tuning learning rate per parameter. Rare-gradient parameters get amplified, jumpy ones damped. AdamW decouples weight decay from this adaptive scaling (decaying weights directly rather than through the gradient), which makes regularization behave consistently — it's the standard for transformers.

Why do transformer schedules use learning-rate warmup?

Early in training, weights are random and Adam's moment estimates are built from a handful of noisy samples — large steps then can catapult the model into poor regions or cause divergence (loss spikes/NaN). Warmup ramps the LR from ~0 over the first steps so statistics stabilize before full-size steps. Empirically transformers are unusually sensitive to this; skipping warmup is a classic cause of failed runs.

How does weight decay reduce overfitting?

It continuously shrinks weights toward zero, so only weights that the training signal repeatedly reinforces stay large. Memorization circuits — supported by one or two examples — get eroded, while broadly-supported general patterns persist. Equivalent to preferring simpler functions (smaller weights → smoother outputs); in AdamW it's applied as a direct multiplicative shrink each step.

Dropout: how does it work at train vs inference time, and why does it help?

Training: each step, zero each activation independently with probability p (and rescale survivors by 1/(1−p) so expected magnitudes match). Inference: disabled entirely — deterministic full network. It helps by preventing co-adaptation (no feature can rely on a specific partner) and by implicitly ensembling the exponentially many sub-networks trained across steps. Watch for it as the classic reason a model behaves differently in model.train() vs model.eval() mode — a real production bug source.

Your loss spikes to NaN mid-training. Give a diagnostic checklist.

(1) Learning rate too high or no warmup — the most common. (2) Exploding gradients — add gradient clipping (standard: clip global norm to 1.0). (3) Numerical issues: fp16 overflow (use bf16 or loss scaling, M7.3), a log(0)/division by zero in custom loss code. (4) Bad batch: corrupted data, extreme outlier, empty sequence. (5) Adam epsilon/beta misconfiguration. Ordered this way, you fix 90% of NaN incidents at step 1 or 2.
