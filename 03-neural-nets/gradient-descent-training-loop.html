The Training Loop

You have all the parts: layers that transform (3.1), a loss that measures surprise (3.2), gradients that point downhill (M1.4). This page assembles them into the loop that every training run in history executes — and decodes the config-file words (batch_size, epochs, learning_rate) you've scrolled past in a hundred Hugging Face tutorials. After this page, a training log reads like a story.

The four-beat loop

forward → loss → backward → step  (repeat)
In plain words: ① push a batch of examples through the model (forward), ② score the wrongness (loss), ③ compute every parameter's blame (backward — next page's magic), ④ nudge all parameters downhill (step). Every model you've ever used is the residue of this loop running millions of times.

The vocabulary, precisely

  • Batch (mini-batch): the handful of examples processed per loop turn — M1.4's "noisy but cheap" slope estimate, now with its official home. Batch loss = average over its examples.
  • Iteration / step: one full turn of the four-beat crank. Training length is often quoted in steps.
  • Epoch: one full pass over the entire training set. dataset_size ÷ batch_size = steps per epoch. Fine-tuning: typically 1–5 epochs. LLM pretraining: often less than one epoch — the corpus is so big they never finish it (and repeating data too much actually hurts).

Batch size trade-offs — the three-way tug-of-war: bigger batches give smoother gradient estimates and better GPU utilization (matrices fill the hardware, M7.1), but cost memory linearly (activations for every example must be held — the OOM you'll meet in M7.2) and, subtly, too-smooth gradients lose the exploration noise that finds flatter, better-generalizing valleys. Common fine-tuning range: 8–64. Pretraining: thousands, with tricks (M7.3) to keep the noise benefits.

The three-way data split, and the sin of leakage

Train (the model learns on it) / validation (you peek during training: tune hyperparameters, decide when to stop) / test (touched once, at the very end, to report honest numbers). Why three and not two? Because every time you make a decision based on validation scores, you leak a little information about it into your choices — after fifty experiments you've quietly overfit to validation. Test stays sealed to keep one number honest.

Leakage — test information contaminating training — is the classic silent catastrophe: duplicate rows across splits, normalizing using statistics computed on all data, splitting time-series randomly (future leaks into past), near-duplicate documents landing on both sides. Symptom: beautiful offline metrics, mysterious production failures. And at LLM scale it has a famous name — benchmark contamination (M11.4): test sets that leaked into pretraining corpora, inflating reported scores. Same sin, bigger stage.

Reading loss curves like a clinician

Real numbers: decoding an actual fine-tune config

A typical HF TrainingArguments: per_device_train_batch_size=32 (32 examples per crank turn), learning_rate=2e-5 (η — tiny, because pretrained weights are already good and big steps would wreck them: M8.1's catastrophic forgetting), num_train_epochs=3 (three full passes; with 10,000 examples that's 10,000÷32 ≈ 313 steps × 3 = 939 total steps), eval_strategy="epoch" (draw the orange curve 3 times), load_best_model_at_end=True (the sweet-spot checkpoint, automated). Every field now maps to a beat of the loop or a point on the curves.

Connect the dots

"Training a model" has probably felt like a ritual performed by other people with bigger GPUs. Deflate it: it's a for loop with four lines in the body. When M9.5 has you fine-tune all-MiniLM on your own RAG pairs, the progress bar you'll watch is this exact loop; the loss it prints is 3.2's cross-entropy cousin; and the decision "2 epochs or 3?" is you looking at this page's two curves. Also: "the model saw this data during training" — the leakage concept — is why you should never evaluate your RAG retriever on chunks you used as fine-tuning positives. Same sin, your stack.

Build: the loop, bare, in 25 lines
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# toy data: 2 classes, 2 features, learnable but noisy
X = np.vstack([rng.normal([-1,-1], 0.7, (200,2)), rng.normal([1,1], 0.7, (200,2))])
y = np.array([0]*200 + [1]*200)
idx = rng.permutation(400)
Xtr, ytr, Xva, yva = X[idx[:300]], y[idx[:300]], X[idx[300:]], y[idx[300:]]

W = rng.normal(size=(2,)) * 0.1; b = 0.0
sigmoid = lambda z: 1/(1+np.exp(-z))
lr, batch = 0.5, 32

for step in range(301):
    i = rng.integers(0, 300, batch)               # grab a mini-batch
    p = sigmoid(Xtr[i] @ W + b)                   # ① forward
    # ② loss (binary cross-entropy) — printed below
    gW = Xtr[i].T @ (p - ytr[i]) / batch          # ③ backward (the (p−y) gradient!)
    gb = (p - ytr[i]).mean()
    W -= lr * gW; b -= lr * gb                    # ④ step
    if step % 50 == 0:
        ptr = sigmoid(Xtr @ W + b); pva = sigmoid(Xva @ W + b)
        ltr = -np.mean(ytr*np.log(ptr+1e-9) + (1-ytr)*np.log(1-ptr+1e-9))
        lva = -np.mean(yva*np.log(pva+1e-9) + (1-yva)*np.log(1-pva+1e-9))
        print(f"step {step:3d}  train {ltr:.3f}  val {lva:.3f}")

Watch both losses fall together (this simple model can't really overfit — too few parameters). Then force the disease: shrink training data to 10 points (idx[:10]) and watch train-loss dive while val-loss climbs — the animation's divergence, reproduced on your machine.

If you remember one thing

Training = forward → loss → backward → step, repeated over mini-batches. Epochs are passes over the data; the config file is loop settings; and the train/validation curve pair is the only honest report card — the moment they diverge, you're memorizing, not learning. Keep test data sealed and leakage-free or every number you report is fiction.

Interview questions

Define epoch, batch, and iteration, and relate them numerically.

Batch: the examples processed in one forward/backward pass. Iteration (step): one such pass plus the parameter update. Epoch: one complete traversal of the training set = dataset_size ÷ batch_size iterations. 50k examples, batch 25 → 2,000 iterations per epoch; 4 epochs → 8,000 steps.

How do you detect overfitting, and name three remedies.

Detection: validation loss rises (or plateaus) while training loss keeps falling — the diverging-curves signature. Remedies: more/better data (strongest), early stopping at the divergence point, regularization (weight decay, dropout — next pages), reducing model capacity, or data augmentation. For fine-tuning specifically: fewer epochs and a smaller learning rate are the first knobs.

Why do you need a test set if you already have validation?

Because validation gets consumed by the experimenter: every hyperparameter chosen to maximize validation score fits noise in that split a little. Across many experiments this compounds — validation becomes optimistically biased. The test set, used once after all decisions are frozen, is the only unbiased estimate. (Kaggle's public-vs-private leaderboard exists precisely because of this effect.)

Give three subtle examples of data leakage.

(1) Preprocessing statistics (normalization means, TF-IDF vocabularies, tokenizer merges) computed on the full dataset before splitting. (2) Near-duplicates straddling the split — common in scraped text; the model "recognizes" test items. (3) Temporal leakage: random splits of time-ordered data let the model train on the future. Bonus, LLM-scale: benchmark test sets present in pretraining crawls — contamination that inflates leaderboard scores (M11.4).

Why is LLM pretraining often less than one epoch?

The corpus (~15T tokens for Llama-3) exceeds what compute budgets can traverse repeatedly, and evidence shows unique data beats repeated data — models start memorizing repeated text, and scaling laws (M7.4) favor spending compute on fresh tokens. So pretraining is one partial pass over a deduplicated corpus, while small-data fine-tuning happily runs multiple epochs.

Batch size: what actually changes when you raise it from 8 to 512?

Gradient noise drops (smoother, more "true" descent direction), GPU throughput rises (bigger matmuls), memory use rises roughly linearly (activations per example), and — subtly — generalization can worsen because the exploration noise that helps find flat minima disappears; large-batch training typically needs learning-rate rescaling and warmup to compensate. It's a systems-and-statistics trade, not a free speedup.
