The Map: What You Know, What's Missing

You've built a RAG system. You've shipped chatbots on top of LLMs. You call all-MiniLM to embed text, GLiNER to pull out entities, and you work alongside agentic tools every day. And years ago you learned bag-of-words, TF-IDF, word frequencies, word2vec.

So here's the strange situation: you can build with these systems, but you can't see inside them. You know that MiniLM turns a sentence into 384 numbers — not how, not why 384, not what would make it better or worse at your task. That's the missing middle, and this course exists to fill it.

The one big realization

MiniLM and the LLM behind your chatbot are not different kinds of things. They are the same invention — the transformer — at different sizes, trained with different objectives. Understand the transformer once and both boxes open with the same key. That's why this course has one core (Module 5) and everything else either leads to it or applies it.

What you already know (both ends)

The classical end. Bag-of-words and TF-IDF turn text into vectors of counts. You already know their weakness: "good" and "great" are as unrelated to each other as "good" and "carburetor" — counting can't see meaning. word2vec was the first fix you met, even if only conceptually. This end gave you the right instinct: text becomes vectors, and vectors get compared.

The application end. RAG, chatbots, embeddings-as-a-service, GLiNER, agent tools. This end gave you the other right instinct: these systems are assembled from callable parts.

What's missing is the part that explains both: how a pile of numbers learns to understand language. Concretely, the middle is:

  • How a model learns anything at all (loss, gradients, backprop — M1, M3)
  • How word vectors are actually trained, not just used (M2)
  • How attention lets a model read in context (M4, M5)
  • The transformer itself, piece by piece, with real numbers (M5)
  • How models are pretrained, fine-tuned, aligned (M6, M8)
  • What all this costs in compute and memory, and why (M7)
  • How your exact tools — all-MiniLM (M9) and GLiNER (M10) — were built
  • How LLMs are sampled, cached, quantized, benchmarked (M11)
  • Your own stack — RAG, reranking, evals, agents — rebuilt from scratch (M12)

How the course is ordered, and why

Strictly bottom-up: every page uses only ideas from earlier pages, and almost every page plants a seed some later page harvests. Real numbers

Amber boxes contain real numbers from real systems — actual model sizes, actual similarity scores, actual GPU memory math. Example, to set the scale of things: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 has ~22.7 million parameters; the original BERT has 110 million; Llama-3-8B has 8 billion; frontier LLMs are in the hundreds of billions to trillions. Same blueprint, five orders of magnitude apart — you'll learn what those extra parameters buy.

Interview warm-up

Every page ends with interview questions. These ones are the kind that open a screening call — make sure you can answer them out loud, not just nod along.

What's the difference between TF-IDF vectors and embedding vectors like MiniLM's?

TF-IDF vectors are sparse counts: one dimension per vocabulary word, values from term frequency × inverse document frequency, no notion of meaning — two synonyms share no signal. Embeddings are dense, learned vectors (e.g. 384 dimensions) trained so that semantically similar text lands close together. TF-IDF measures word overlap; embeddings measure meaning overlap. (You'll be able to explain how that training works after M2 and M9.)

Your RAG system returns bad chunks. Name the two most likely stages to investigate.

(1) Retrieval: the embedding model doesn't rank truly relevant chunks near the query — wrong model for the domain, or chunks poorly cut. (2) Chunking: chunks too big (diluted embeddings) or too small (context split apart). The course gives you the tools to measure which one is failing (recall@k, M9/M12) instead of guessing.

Is the model behind ChatGPT-style assistants fundamentally different from an embedding model like MiniLM?

No — both are transformers. MiniLM is a small encoder trained to produce one representative vector per text; chat LLMs are large decoders trained to predict the next token, then tuned to follow instructions. Same core mechanism (self-attention), different size, objective and head.
