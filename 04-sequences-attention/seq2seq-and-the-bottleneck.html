Seq2Seq & the Bottleneck

2014: the field wants machine translation — a sequence in, a different sequence out. The architecture invented for it, seq2seq, introduced two characters who star in everything since: the encoder and the decoder. It also contained a flaw so visible, so measurable, that fixing it produced attention itself. This page is the setup for that punchline.

Encoder reads, decoder writes

The design: one RNN (the encoder) reads the entire source sentence and hands over its final hidden state — one vector meant to contain the whole meaning. A second RNN (the decoder) takes that vector and generates the target sentence from it, token by token (M4.1's chain rule at work). Two specialists: a reader that compresses, a writer that expands.

The bottleneck, measured

You can already predict the failure from M4.2: the final hidden state over-represents the end of the sentence and dilutes the beginning. For a 40-word sentence, early words pass through 40 lossy updates before the decoder ever starts. Why 2015-era products felt the way they did

If you used translation tools or the first neural chatbots around then, you experienced this page: crisp short sentences, and long inputs that came back generic, hedged, or with the second half of your sentence simply ignored. Products were quietly built around the flaw — split long sentences, keep replies short. The bottleneck wasn't a bug in someone's code; it was the architecture's information budget, visible to end users.

Real numbers

Budget arithmetic: a 1,000-dim fp32 hidden state = 4 KB to carry a whole sentence. A 50-word German sentence with case markings, clause structure and three named entities simply exceeds 4 KB of faithful summary — while the decoder needs specific words back out, not gist. Compare attention's budget (next page): the decoder keeps access to all 50 encoder states — 200 KB — a 50× bigger information channel, priced per-use instead of prepaid.

Connect the dots

You have felt this exact failure in your own systems, one layer up: cram twenty documents into a single summary and ask the LLM to answer from the summary — details are gone; the answer hedges. Your fix was RAG: keep the documents, retrieve the relevant ones at question time. That is precisely the 2014→2015 move: stop pre-compressing everything into one blob; give the consumer selective access to the originals. Attention is RAG inside a sentence — a line to keep for the next page, where it becomes literal.

If you remember one thing

Seq2seq split the world into encoder (reads) and decoder (writes) — vocabulary that survives to this day — but forced everything through one fixed-size vector. The result was a measurable quality cliff with sentence length. The fix must let the decoder look back at all encoder states, selectively, at every step. Name that mechanism and you've named the next page.

Interview questions

What is the seq2seq bottleneck, precisely?

In encoder–decoder RNNs, the source sequence's entire meaning must pass through the encoder's final fixed-size hidden state. Information scales with sentence length; the channel doesn't. Long inputs get lossy-compressed — early tokens most of all — and translation quality degrades sharply with length (the Bahdanau 2014 curve). It's an information-capacity limit, not a training deficiency: more data can't widen the pipe.

Where do the terms "encoder" and "decoder" come from and why do they persist?

From seq2seq translation: encoder maps input sequence → representation; decoder maps representation → output sequence. They persist because the decomposition is architecture-independent: BERT is an encoder used alone (understanding), GPT a decoder used alone (generation), T5/translation models use both with cross-attention (M5.8). Interviewers expect you to map model families onto these two roles fluently.

Why does the final RNN state over-represent the end of the input?

Recency by construction: each update h_t = f(h_{t−1}, x_t) mixes the new token in while diluting the accumulated summary; a token k steps back has been through k such dilutions. Backward, gradients from the handoff reach recent tokens through short chains and distant ones through long, vanishing ones (M3.4). Both directions bias toward the end — the sentence's beginning is oldest news in the summary.

Design question: without knowing about attention, how would you fix the bottleneck?

Natural proposals, and their verdicts: bigger hidden state (helps constants, not scaling — capacity still fixed); read the input backwards or bidirectionally (helps which end is fresh, not total capacity); hand the decoder ALL encoder states (right idea! — but it needs a way to select relevant ones per output step); let the decoder query those states with learned relevance weights per step — congratulations, you've derived attention. This "invent it yourself" path is exactly how the question is used in interviews.
