RNNs & LSTMs, Briefly

Deliberately short page. RNNs ruled NLP from ~2013 to 2017, and you need them for exactly two reasons: their failures are the design requirements of the transformer, and "why did attention replace RNNs?" is a top-five interview question. We take what we need and move on.

The idea: read in order, carry a memory

M3's networks took fixed-size inputs. Language is a sequence of arbitrary length — so the recurrent network reads one token at a time, maintaining a single hidden state vector: a running summary of everything read so far. At each step: new state = f(old state, current token) — same weights reused at every position, like one reader moving along the line, updating their mental notes.

Why it breaks: two structural diseases

Disease 1 — long-range decay. You already own the math: backprop through a 100-word sentence means blame flowing through 100 chained steps — M3.4's shrinking-bars animation with the layers laid horizontally. Gradients vanish over distance, so the model literally cannot learn dependencies between far-apart words, and the forward pass has the matching problem you just watched: fixed-size memory diluted by every update.

Disease 2 — enforced serialism. h₅ cannot be computed before h₄. Training can't parallelize across the sequence: a 1,000-token document is a 1,000-step chain, no matter how many GPU cores idle. M1.3 taught that hardware loves one giant matrix multiply; RNNs structurally forbid it. This — as much as memory decay — is what killed them: they couldn't scale into the data.

LSTMs: brilliant patch, same wall

The LSTM (1997, dominant 2014–2017) patched disease 1 with gates: small learned valves deciding, at each step, what to write into memory, what to erase, and what to expose — plus a protected "cell state" lane that information can ride with less mangling (a cousin of the residual highways coming in M5.5). GRUs are the simplified two-gate version. The gates genuinely helped: dependencies of 50–100 tokens became learnable, and LSTMs powered Google Translate circa 2016.

But gates only slow the decay — memory is still one fixed-size vector updated serially — and they do nothing at all for disease 2. The field needed random access to the past, computed in parallel. That requirement has a name, and it's the next two pages.

Real numbers

Google's 2016 neural translation system: 8 stacked LSTM layers, trained for about a week on ~96 GPUs — for one bilingual pair. The 2017 transformer paper's base model matched/beat it after 12 hours on 8 GPUs — roughly a 100× drop in training cost, delivered almost entirely by parallelism (disease 2 cured). Quality won headlines; trainability at scale won the war.

Connect the dots

A fixed-size state that must summarize an unbounded past — you've engineered around this exact shape: a chatbot session where you keep only a rolling summary of the conversation, because you can't resend everything. You know from experience what it forgets: specifics from twenty turns ago. That's an RNN's life at every single token. And the fix you'd instinctively reach for — "just let me look back at the full transcript when needed" — is precisely attention. You've already invented it; the next pages just add matrices.

If you remember one thing

RNNs read serially into a fixed-size memory: information decays with distance (vanishing gradients, forward dilution) and computation can't parallelize. LSTMs' gates eased the decay but kept the serial bottleneck. Requirements for a successor: direct access to any past position + parallel computation over the whole sequence. Hold those two; they define everything that follows.

Interview questions

Why did transformers replace RNNs/LSTMs? (the canonical question)

Two structural RNN flaws: (1) long-range information must survive sequential compression through a fixed-size hidden state — gradients vanish and memories dilute over distance; (2) computation is inherently serial across time steps, so training can't exploit GPU parallelism over the sequence. Attention gives every position direct, learned access to every other position, computed as parallel matrix multiplies — fixing memory and scalability in one move. The 100× training-cost drop did the rest.

What problem do LSTM gates solve, and what do they NOT solve?

Gates (input/forget/output) learn what to write, erase and expose in a protected cell state, letting gradients survive tens of steps instead of a handful — solving much of the vanishing-gradient problem within moderate ranges. They do NOT remove the fixed-capacity bottleneck (one vector still summarizes everything) and do NOT enable parallel training across time. Patch, not cure.

Are RNNs completely dead in 2020s practice?

Mostly for NLP, but not universally: they persist in low-latency streaming settings (tiny per-step cost, O(1) state), some time-series work, and their ideas returned refreshed in modern state-space models (Mamba-family), which offer RNN-like O(n) inference with parallelizable training — the current research challenger to attention's O(n²) (context: M11.2). Mentioning that arc marks you as current rather than dismissive.

An RNN and a transformer both fail on a long document. How do their failure modes differ?

RNN: graceful-but-early degradation — distant information fades continuously; there's no hard limit but effective memory is short. Transformer: sharp boundary — perfect random access within the context window, total amnesia beyond it (plus O(n²) cost growth and some mid-context dilution, M11.2). Summarize-as-you-go vs open-book-with-a-page-limit.
