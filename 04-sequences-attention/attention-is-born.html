Attention Is Born

2015. The bottleneck is on the table, and the fix — from Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio — is disarmingly direct: stop forcing the decoder to live off one vector; let it look back at every encoder state, every time it writes a word. This page is that mechanism, plus the vocabulary shift (query, keys, values) that turns it into the seed of the entire transformer. Read slowly: this is the load-bearing idea of the whole course.

The move: weighted glances instead of one memory

Keep the encoder exactly as it was — but keep all of its per-word hidden states instead of discarding everything but the last. Then, each time the decoder is about to produce a word, it: ① scores every encoder state for relevance to what it's trying to write right now — using the dot product, M1.2's agreement meter; ② softmaxes those scores into weights (M3.2 — differentiable choosing!); ③ takes the weighted average of all encoder states as its context for this one word. Fresh glance, every word.

The soft lookup, and why "soft" matters

Notice what attention really is: a lookup into a set of vectors, by content. A hard lookup ("take state #2") would be undifferentiable — you can't backprop through a discrete choice (M3.2's cliff problem). Attention's genius is making the lookup soft: take a little of everything, weighted by relevance. Softmax makes the weights nearly-hard when the model is confident (0.74 vs 0.02s) while keeping slopes alive everywhere — so gradient descent can literally teach the model where to look. Differentiable choosing, applied to memory access: that's the whole invention.

The vocabulary that unlocks Module 5: query, keys, values

Rename the three roles in what you just watched, because these names run the rest of the course:

  • The decoder's "what am I trying to write right now?" state — the thing doing the asking — is a query.
  • Each encoder state, in its role of being scored for relevance, acts as a key.
  • Each encoder state, in its role of contributing content to the weighted average, acts as a value.
attention(q, keys, values) = Σᵢ softmax(q · kᵢ) · vᵢ
In plain words: score the query against every key (dot products), soften into weights, blend the values accordingly. In 2015's version, key and value were the same vector wearing two hats. Module 5's twist — worth a one-sentence spoiler — is to give each token separately learned q, k and v vectors, and to let a sentence attend to itself. That twist plus M3's toolkit = the transformer.

And the 2017 question practically asks itself. If attention gives direct access to any position (killing the memory problem), and it's built from dot products and softmaxes (parallelizable matrix math, killing the serial problem — M4.2's two diseases both cured)… why keep the RNN at all? The paper that asked it out loud was titled "Attention Is All You Need." Module 5 is what they built.

Real numbers

From the 2014–15 benchmark that settled it: on sentences of 50+ words, fixed-vector seq2seq lost roughly 10 BLEU points versus its short-sentence performance, while the attention model's curve stayed flat — and attention added only ~1–2% more parameters. The alignment weights came out interpretable for free: plotted as a grid, they recover word-alignment tables linguists used to build by hand — including the diagonal flips where German word order inverts English's.

Connect the dots

Last page promised: attention is RAG inside a sentence. Now it's literal — map the pieces: query = your user's embedded question; keys = your chunk embeddings in the vector DB; scoring = the same dot product; top-k retrieval = a hard version of the softmax weighting; values = the chunk texts handed to the LLM. You built decoder-side attention at system scale without knowing its name. Difference: your RAG's "attention weights" are fixed by MiniLM's geometry, while the transformer learns its glancing strategy end-to-end for the task. That difference is why M5 needs three separate matrices — see you there.

Build: attention in eight lines of numpy
import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())
    return e / e.sum()

# 5 encoder states (one per German word), 4-D each — toy numbers
states = np.array([[0.1, 0.9, 0.2, 0.0],    # Die
                   [0.8, 0.1, 0.9, 0.2],    # Katze  ← cat-like content
                   [0.2, 0.7, 0.1, 0.9],    # jagte
                   [0.1, 0.8, 0.3, 0.1],    # den
                   [0.9, 0.2, 0.8, 0.1]])   # Hund

query = np.array([0.9, 0.0, 0.8, 0.1])      # decoder about to write "cat"

scores  = states @ query                     # ① relevance: 5 dot products (M1.2)
weights = softmax(scores)                    # ② differentiable choosing (M3.2)
context = weights @ states                   # ③ weighted average of values

print("weights:", weights.round(3))          # Katze & Hund light up (both animals!)
print("context:", context.round(3))

Eight lines, and they are the exact skeleton of what runs billions of times per second inside every LLM. Note the "bug" in the output — Hund scores high too, because this toy query only knows "animal-ness". Distinguishing which animal needs richer, learned projections… which is the first thing M5.3 fixes.

If you remember one thing

Attention = score with dot products, soften with softmax, blend the values — a differentiable, learnable lookup that gives the writer direct access to everything the reader saw. It cured the bottleneck, its math is parallel, and renaming its parts (query, keys, values) hands you the keys to the transformer. You now hold the single most important idea in modern AI.

Interview questions

Explain attention (the 2015 kind) in three sentences.

Instead of compressing the source into one vector, keep every encoder state. At each decoding step, score all encoder states against the decoder's current state (dot products), softmax the scores into weights, and use the weighted average as that step's context. The scoring is differentiable, so where-to-look is learned by ordinary backprop.

Why must attention be "soft" (a weighted average) rather than picking the best state?

A hard argmax selection has zero gradient — you can't learn where to look through a non-differentiable choice (same reason accuracy can't be a loss, M3.2). The softmax-weighted blend keeps every path differentiable while approaching hard selection as the model grows confident. It also degrades gracefully: when several positions matter, the blend represents that naturally.

Define query, key and value in one line each, with the retrieval analogy.

Query: the representation of what I'm looking for right now (the search box). Key: each candidate's advertisement, matched against the query to produce a relevance score (the index entry). Value: the content actually retrieved and blended once relevance is decided (the document). In 2015 attention, keys and values were the same encoder states; transformers learn separate projections for the three roles (M5.3).

How did attention solve BOTH of the RNN's structural problems?

Memory: any position is one dot product away from any other — no information relay across timesteps, so no decay with distance; the length-vs-quality cliff flattens. Parallelism: scores and blends are matrix multiplications over all positions at once — no sequential dependency in the attention computation itself. That double cure is precisely what made "drop the RNN entirely" (the 2017 transformer) plausible.

What's the computational cost of attention over a sequence of length n, and why will it matter later?

Every query attends over n keys; with n queries (self-attention, M5.3) that's O(n²) score computations — quadratic in sequence length. It's the price of all-pairs direct access, and it's exactly why context windows are finite, why long-context inference is expensive, and why KV caches and attention variants exist (M11.2). Knowing that the field's flagship limitation traces to this one design choice is senior-level context.
