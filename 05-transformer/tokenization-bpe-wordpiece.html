Tokenization: BPE & WordPiece

The transformer module begins at the loading dock: before any attention happens, text must become a sequence of IDs from a fixed vocabulary. That conversion — tokenization — quietly explains a dozen oddities you've met in production: why token counts never match word counts, why models fumble counting letters in "strawberry", why non-English text costs more, and why your RAG chunker measures tokens. All of it falls out of one greedy little algorithm.

Why not words? Why not characters?

Words fail for the reason M2.4 taught: fixed vocabularies meet unlimited language. "Kubernetes", "COVID-19", typos, German compound nouns — all out-of-vocabulary, all mapped to a useless <UNK>. Characters fail the opposite way: "understanding" becomes 13 steps of nearly-meaningless input, sequences balloon (remember attention's O(n²) bill from M4.4), and the model wastes capacity re-learning that t-h-e spells a word. The answer sits between: subwords — frequent words stay whole, rare words split into meaningful, reusable pieces. fastText's n-gram insight (M2.4), industrialized.

BPE: the merge game

Byte-Pair Encoding builds the vocabulary bottom-up from data. Start with single characters. Then repeat one move: find the most frequent adjacent pair of tokens in the corpus, merge it into a new token, add it to the vocabulary. Run 30,000–100,000 merges. That's the entire algorithm:

Real tokenizers run this on hundreds of gigabytes, producing merge tables like: rank 1 "t"+"h", rank 2 "th"+"e"… down to rank 50,000. At inference, text is tokenized by replaying merges in rank order — deterministic and fast. Two details worth knowing by name: byte-level BPE (GPT-family) starts from raw bytes, so any string — emoji, Klingon, binary garbage — is tokenizable; WordPiece (BERT/MiniLM family) is the same shape but merges the pair with highest likelihood gain rather than raw count, and marks word-continuations with ## ("unhappiness" → un ##happi ##ness). SentencePiece treats spaces as just another symbol (the ▁ you see in Llama tokens), making it language-agnostic. Same family, different accents.

The consequences you've already met

Real numbers

Same sentence — "The transformer architecture revolutionized NLP" — through three real tokenizers: GPT-4's cl100k_base (100k vocab): 8 tokens; GPT-2 (50k): 9 tokens; BERT WordPiece (30k): 10 tokens including ##ized. Rule-of-thumb economics: 1 token ≈ 4 characters ≈ 0.75 English words. Vocab-size trade-off in one line: bigger vocab → shorter sequences (cheaper attention) but a bigger embedding table — 100k tokens × 4096 dims ≈ 400M parameters before the first transformer layer even runs.

Connect the dots

Look at MiniLM's config with today's eyes: "vocab_size": 30522 — that's a WordPiece vocabulary, and it's why M1.3's parameter audit found an 11.7M-parameter embedding table (30,522 × 384). When your RAG splitter is set to "512 tokens max", that's MiniLM's max_position_embeddings — next page explains that number. Build: dissect three tokenizers on your own prompts
# pip install tiktoken transformers
import tiktoken
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

gpt4 = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")
bert = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

for text in ["The transformer architecture revolutionized NLP",
             "unhappiness", "strawberry", "Kubernetes pod eviction",
             "ಕನ್ನಡ ಭಾಷೆ"]:                       # try your own languages!
    g = [gpt4.decode([t]) for t in gpt4.encode(text)]
    b = bert.tokenize(text)
    print(f"\n{text!r}")
    print(f"  GPT-4 ({len(g)} tok): {g}")
    print(f"  BERT  ({len(b)} tok): {b}")

Paste in a real prompt from your chatbot and a chunk from your RAG corpus. Finding a domain term that shatters into 6 tokens tells you something practical: that term's meaning is being assembled from pieces every single call — one reason domain fine-tuning (M8/M9) helps.

If you remember one thing

Tokenization = a learned compression dictionary: greedy merges of frequent pairs, giving frequent words one ID and spelling everything else from pieces — OOV eliminated by construction. Models see only these IDs (never letters), all limits and prices are denominated in them, and the tokenizer's training data decides who pays more. Interview questions

Why do all modern LLMs use subword tokenization rather than words or characters?

Words: unbounded vocabulary → OOV failures and giant embedding tables. Characters: guaranteed coverage but sequences 4–5× longer (quadratic attention cost) and semantically empty units. Subwords interpolate: ~30k–100k units where frequent words stay whole and rare words decompose into meaningful, reusable pieces — full coverage, moderate lengths, morphology captured for free.

Walk through the BPE training algorithm and how inference-time tokenization works.

Training: initialize the vocab with characters (or bytes); repeatedly count all adjacent token pairs in the corpus, merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol, record the merge; stop at the target vocab size. Inference: split text to characters, then apply the recorded merges in learned priority order until no more apply. Deterministic, fast, and the merge table IS the tokenizer.

Why can't LLMs reliably count letters or reverse words?

They never observe characters — input is subword IDs, and "berry" is a single opaque embedding with no internal letter structure exposed. Character-level facts are only learnable indirectly (from spelling-related text in training data), which is unreliable. Tasks that force per-character access (counting, reversing, acrostics) probe exactly the representation gap the tokenizer created. Fixes: tool use (let it call code), or character-level prompting tricks.

What are the trade-offs in choosing vocabulary size?

Larger vocab: shorter sequences (cheaper O(n²) attention, more content per context window) and whole-word tokens for more terms, but a bigger embedding table (vocab × d parameters — can dwarf small models), rarer tokens get under-trained embeddings, and softmax over the vocab costs more. Smaller vocab: the reverse. Sweet spots have drifted up with model size: 30k (BERT) → 50k (GPT-2) → 100k+ (GPT-4) → 128k–256k (multilingual frontier models, partly for language equity).

Your multilingual users report higher costs and worse quality than English users. Explain via tokenization.

The tokenizer's merges were learned on training data where English dominates, so English gets efficient long merges while other scripts fragment into many short tokens — the same content costs 2–4× more tokens (direct pricing impact), consumes context window faster, and gives the model fewer, noisier units to reason over (quality impact). Mitigations: models with multilingual-balanced tokenizers, or language-specific models. This is a great interview answer because it connects an invisible preprocessing choice to user-visible cost and fairness.
