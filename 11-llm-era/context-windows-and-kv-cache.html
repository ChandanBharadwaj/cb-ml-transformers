Context Windows & the KV Cache

Three mysteries from your daily LLM life, one mechanism behind all of them: why is there a context limit at all? Why does the first token take seconds while the rest stream fast (M5.7 promised this answer)? And why do long conversations get slower and pricier as they grow? The answers are, in order: attention is quadratic, the KV cache exists, and the KV cache is enormous.

Why context is finite: the n² wall

Self-attention's power is that every token attends to every other token (M5.3). Its price is that every token attends to every other token: n tokens means an n×n attention matrix, per head, per layer. Double the context, quadruple the attention work. 128k tokens of context means each n×n matrix has 16 billion cells' worth of score computations across the stack. The context window isn't a product decision someone typed into a config — it's where quadratic cost (and, as you'll see, cache memory) stops being worth paying.

The KV cache: never compute the past twice

Now recall generation (M5.7): one new token per forward pass, appended, repeat. Naively, generating token 1,001 means re-encoding all 1,000 previous tokens — re-computing their K and V vectors that haven't changed (the causal mask guarantees past tokens can't see the new one, so their representations are frozen). The fix is obvious once seen: store every token's K and V the first time, and each new step computes only the new token's Q, K, V — one fresh query against a growing library of cached keys and values. Generation drops from quadratic-per-token to linear-per-token. This single optimization is why chat is usable at all — and its memory bill is the plot twist of this page.

KV bytes = 2 · layers · kv_heads · head_dim · 2 bytes · n_tokens

In words: two vectors (K and V), for each layer, for each KV head, of head_dim numbers at 2 bytes each (fp16), for every token in context. Llama-3-8B: 2 × 32 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 dims × 2 bytes = 131 KB per token. Multiply by your context length and that's VRAM someone is paying for while your chat sits open.

How 128k contexts became possible anyway

Three tricks, each one intuition deep. GQA (grouped-query attention): let groups of query heads share one K/V head — Llama-3-8B has 32 query heads but only 8 KV heads, shrinking the cache 4× with barely any quality loss (that "kv_heads = 8" in the formula above is GQA; MQA is the extreme: one shared KV head). RoPE scaling: because RoPE encodes relative position as rotation angles (M5.2), the angle schedule can be stretched or interpolated after training to address positions the model never saw — long-context fine-tunes start here. Sliding-window attention: each token attends only to its last w neighbors (Mistral's w = 4,096), making cost linear — information still travels far by hopping window-to-window across layers, like a relay.

Lost in the middle: big windows ≠ even attention

One honest caveat before you trust a 128k window: retrieval-style tests (a "needle" fact hidden at varying depths in a long context) show a stubborn U-shape — models recall the beginning and end of context well and sag in the middle. Attention mass dilutes across thousands of tokens, and training data had more beginnings and ends than middles. Practical rule for your RAG prompts: put the highest-ranked retrieved chunks first (or first and last), never buried mid-prompt in retrieval order — placement is a free accuracy boost.

Real numbers

Llama-3-8B, fp16, 8k context, batch of one: model weights 16 GB + KV cache ~1 GB. Serve 24 concurrent chats and the caches alone need ~25 GB — more than the model. That arithmetic is why GQA exists (without it: ×4, ~100 GB), why vLLM's PagedAttention treats KV memory like virtual-memory pages (M7.5's continuous batching, completed), and why API providers price long context so aggressively: your 100k-token conversation is squatting on ~13 GB of someone's H100.

Connect the dots

This page is three of your modules colliding: M5.7's autoregressive loop (why a cache helps), M5.2's RoPE (why context can stretch), M7.2's memory math (what it costs). And it hands you two production habits: your RAG system exists partly because stuffing whole document sets into context is quadratic-expensive and lossy-in-the-middle — retrieval is the cure, and chunk placement in the prompt is part of the dose.

Build: a KV-cache calculator from config.json
def kv_cache_gb(layers, kv_heads, head_dim, n_tokens, bytes_per=2):
    per_token = 2 * layers * kv_heads * head_dim * bytes_per
    print(f"{per_token/1024:.0f} KB/token")
    return per_token * n_tokens / 1e9

# numbers straight from each model's config.json (M5.6 skill):

print(kv_cache_gb(32, 32, 128, 8_192))    # same but no GQA   → ~4.3 GB
print(kv_cache_gb(80, 8, 128, 128_000))   # Llama-3-70B, 128k → ~42 GB

Then open any model's config.json on Hugging Face (num_hidden_layers, num_key_value_heads, head_dim or hidden_size ÷ heads) and price its cache at the context you actually use. Compare num_attention_heads vs num_key_value_heads — the GQA ratio, visible in the wild.

If you remember one thing

Attention is quadratic in context, so past tokens' K and V are computed once and cached; prefill fills the cache (slow first token), decode reuses it (fast streaming), and the cache's size — ~128 KB per token for an 8B model — is the real reason long context is expensive. GQA shrinks it, RoPE scaling stretches positions, and the middle of a long context is where facts go to be forgotten — so put your best chunks at the edges.

Interview questions

What exactly is stored in the KV cache, and why not Q?

For every generated-or-prompted token, each layer's K and V vectors (per KV head) are stored. Q is not cached because it's only ever used once: token i's query attends to the past at step i and never again — future tokens attend with their OWN queries against everyone's keys. K and V, by contrast, are needed by every future step. The causal mask is what makes caching sound: past tokens can't attend to the future, so their K/V never change once computed.

Explain prefill vs decode, and why time-to-first-token scales with prompt length but streaming speed doesn't.

Prefill processes the entire prompt in one parallel pass — all positions at once, building the full KV cache; it's compute-bound and its cost grows with prompt length (quadratically in the attention terms), which is exactly the time-to-first-token you feel. Decode then generates one token per pass, each computing only the new token's QKV and one attention row against the cache; it's memory-bandwidth-bound (streaming weights and cache from VRAM), so per-token latency is roughly constant regardless of how long the prompt was. Same split as M7.5's economics: input tokens are cheap parallel work, output tokens are serial bandwidth.

Compute the KV cache size for a 32-layer model with 8 KV heads of dimension 128 at 32k context, fp16. What would it be without GQA (32 KV heads)?

Per token: 2 (K and V) × 32 layers × 8 heads × 128 dims × 2 bytes = 131 KB. At 32k tokens: ~4.3 GB per sequence. Without GQA (32 KV heads): ×4 = 524 KB/token, ~17 GB per sequence — likely exceeding what's left on the GPU after weights. This ×4 is why grouped-query attention became standard: query diversity matters more than key/value diversity, so sharing KV heads costs little quality and buys enormous serving capacity.

What is "lost in the middle," and how does it change how you build RAG prompts?

Needle-in-a-haystack evaluations show recall of a fact depends on its position in context: high at the start and end, sagging in the middle — a U-curve that persists even in models with huge advertised windows. Causes: attention dilution over thousands of positions and training-data bias toward useful beginnings/ends. RAG implications: don't dump chunks in arbitrary order — place the top-ranked chunks at the start (and optionally repeat crucial ones at the end near the question); don't equate "fits in context" with "will be used" — retrieval + a modest window often beats context-stuffing on both accuracy and cost.

How do sliding-window attention and RoPE scaling each extend usable context, and what do they trade away?

Sliding window: each token attends only to the previous w tokens, making attention O(n·w) instead of O(n²) and capping per-token cache reads; long-range information must relay across layers (layer 1 sees w back, layer 2 effectively 2w, etc.), so very distant precise recall weakens — a compute/recall trade. RoPE scaling: since RoPE encodes relative positions as rotation angles, interpolating or rescaling frequencies (position interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, YaRN) maps unseen long positions into the trained angle range, usually with a short long-context fine-tune; the trade is some resolution — positions get "squeezed," which can subtly blur fine positional distinctions. Production models stack these: GQA for cache, RoPE scaling for range, plus strong long-context training data.
