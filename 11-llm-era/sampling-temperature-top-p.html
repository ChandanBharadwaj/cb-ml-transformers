Sampling: Temperature & Top-p

You've typed temperature=0.7, top_p=0.9 into API calls for years — numbers copied from someone's blog post, adjusted by vibes. This module opens with the end of that cargo cult. You already own every piece: the model outputs a probability distribution over the next token (M4.1), produced by a softmax (M3.2). Sampling parameters don't touch the model at all — they reshape that final distribution before one token is drawn from it. That's the whole secret.

Greedy decoding, and why it repeats itself

The obvious strategy — always take the highest-probability token — is called greedy decoding. It sounds optimal and isn't, for two reasons. First, locally-best tokens don't compose into globally-best text (picking the single most likely next word at every step is how you get the blandest possible sentence). Second, and famously: greedy loops. A phrase that was likely once is likely again after it appears, and the model spirals — "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry." Randomness isn't a bug tolerance in LLMs; a controlled amount is required for text that sounds alive. The question is how much, and that's exactly what your two dials control.

Temperature: the softmax divisor you already met

M3.2 planted this flag and now we collect it: temperature is a number that divides every logit before the softmax. Dividing by T < 1 stretches the gaps between scores — the winner's lead grows, probability concentrates, output becomes focused and repeatable. Dividing by T > 1 compresses the gaps — underdogs get real probability mass, output diversifies and, pushed too far, dissolves into word salad. At T → 0 the distribution collapses onto the single top token: greedy, as a limiting case.

P(token i) = e^(zi/T) / Σj e^(zj/T)

In words: the same softmax from M3.2 with one knob added — divide every raw score z by T first. Small T exaggerates score differences (sharp, confident distribution); large T shrinks them (flat, adventurous distribution). The model's opinion never changes — only how strictly you enforce it at the draw.

Top-k and top-p: two ways to cut the tail

Temperature has a flaw: raising it feeds probability to everything, including the genuinely absurd tail — a 50k-token vocabulary has a lot of "baguette"s. So we cut the tail before drawing. Top-k keeps the k highest-probability tokens, always exactly k — rigid, blind to context. Top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p — a waterline, not a headcount. That adaptivity is why top-p won: when the model is certain (one token holds 0.95), the nucleus is 1–2 tokens; when many continuations are legitimate (the start of a story), the nucleus opens up to dozens. The cut adapts to the model's own confidence, per token.

The remaining dials, briefly

Frequency penalty subtracts from a token's logit each time it has already appeared (fights repetition); presence penalty subtracts a flat amount once a token has appeared at all (nudges toward new topics). Both operate on logits before softmax, like temperature. They're band-aids for the echo problem greedy decoding made famous — small values (0.1–0.5) usually suffice, and reasoning-heavy tasks generally want them at 0 (repeating an equation isn't a bug).

Real numbers

Working defaults, by task: extraction / classification / code: T = 0–0.2, top_p = 1 (you want the model's honest argmax — your GLiNER threshold instinct applies); chat / RAG answers: T = 0.7, top_p = 0.9–0.95 (industry default for a reason — alive but grounded); brainstorming: T = 1.0–1.3, top_p = 0.95 (temperature feeds the tail, top-p keeps it from going feral). And one myth killed: T = 0 is not perfectly deterministic in production APIs — batching order and floating-point non-associativity on GPUs (M7.1) still wiggle results. Treat determinism as a strong tendency, not a contract.

Connect the dots

Every knob on this page acts on the output of M3.2's softmax over M4.1's next-token distribution — machinery you can now draw from memory. Your RAG chatbot almost certainly wants two different settings you may have been sharing: near-zero temperature for anything parsed downstream (JSON, citations, tool arguments) and ~0.7 for the conversational answer itself. If you've ever seen your bot cite a document that doesn't exist at T = 1.0, you've watched the tail get sampled.

Build: the drift grid
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer
import torch

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")

msgs = [{"role": "user", "content": "Describe coffee in one sentence."}]
ids = tok.apply_chat_template(msgs, return_tensors="pt", add_generation_prompt=True)

for T in [0.2, 0.7, 1.3]:
    for p in [0.5, 0.95]:
        torch.manual_seed(0)
        out = model.generate(ids, do_sample=True, temperature=T, top_p=p,
                             max_new_tokens=40, pad_token_id=tok.eos_token_id)
        print(f"T={T} p={p}: {tok.decode(out[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True)}")

Run the 3×2 grid and read it like the animations: down the temperature axis the wording loosens; tightening top_p at high temperature reins the chaos back in. Then repeat with a factual prompt ("What is the capital of France?") and watch high temperature start gambling with the answer itself.

If you remember one thing

The model always outputs the same thing: a full probability distribution over its vocabulary. Temperature reshapes that distribution (divide logits by T inside the softmax); top-p trims its tail adaptively; then one token is drawn. Low T for anything a program will parse, ~0.7 for humans, and never confuse sampling randomness with model knowledge — the ranking never moved.

Interview questions

Why does greedy decoding produce repetitive text, and why does sampling fix it?

Greedy takes the argmax at every step. Two failure modes: (1) locally-optimal tokens don't compose into globally good text — beam search partially addresses this for closed tasks like translation; (2) degenerate loops: once a phrase appears, its tokens get probability boosts in similar contexts, and argmax deterministically re-enters the same cycle ("I'm sorry. I'm sorry."). Sampling breaks the cycle because a random draw eventually escapes the loop's basin. The deeper point (Holtzman's nucleus-sampling paper): human text is NOT a maximum-likelihood object — real language constantly uses "surprising" tokens, so decoding must preserve some entropy to sound human.

Mechanically, what does temperature do? Why does T→0 approximate greedy?

Temperature divides every logit before the softmax: P = softmax(z/T). For T<1, differences between logits are amplified, so the exp() gap between winner and rest explodes and mass concentrates on the top token; for T>1, differences shrink and the distribution flattens toward uniform. As T→0 the largest logit's advantage becomes infinite and softmax converges to a one-hot on the argmax — greedy decoding as a limit. Crucially it's a monotone transform: the ranking of tokens never changes, only the sharpness of the distribution around that ranking.

Top-k vs top-p: what's the difference and why did top-p become the default?

Top-k keeps a fixed number of candidates; top-p keeps the smallest prefix of the sorted distribution whose cumulative probability ≥ p. Top-k's flaw is rigidity: on a confident distribution k=50 admits 48 junk tokens; on a genuinely open one it may exclude legitimate candidates. Top-p adapts per token to the model's own uncertainty — tiny nucleus when confident, wide when not. Since the whole point of tail-cutting is "match the candidate set to the actual uncertainty," the adaptive rule wins. In practice they can be combined (top-k as a hard safety cap, top-p inside it).

Is temperature=0 deterministic? Answer like someone who has run production LLMs.

Mostly, not exactly. T=0 (or greedy) fixes the sampling rule, but production non-determinism leaks in elsewhere: floating-point addition is non-associative and GPU kernel scheduling varies, so logits can differ in the last bits between runs; continuous batching means your request shares kernels with different neighbors each time; ties in the top logit break arbitrarily; and MoE models add expert-routing variance under batching. When two tokens sit within float-noise of each other, a one-token flip early can cascade into a visibly different completion. Treat T=0 as "minimum variance," and design downstream parsing to tolerate variation rather than assuming replay-exactness.

Your RAG bot occasionally cites a document that doesn't exist. Which sampling settings do you inspect first, and why?

Temperature and top-p on the answer-generation call. Fabricated citations are classic tail-sampling: the model's distribution over "next citation token" has real mass on plausible-but-fake IDs, and high T or loose top-p lets that mass get drawn. Fixes in order: T→0–0.2 for any structured/citation-bearing output; constrain the format (make the model choose from an explicit list of retrieved doc IDs placed in the prompt — turning generation into selection); separate calls — creative wording at 0.7 for prose, near-greedy for the citation block; and add a post-hoc check that every cited ID exists in the retrieved set (a grounding check, formalized in 11.6/12.3). Sampling isn't the root cause of hallucination — but it's the cheapest dial that reduces its rate.
