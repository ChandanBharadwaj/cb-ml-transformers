Reasoning & Test-Time Compute

Every model picker you use now has a fork in it: the fast model, or the "thinking" one that sits silent for thirty seconds and costs several times more. This page explains what that second option actually does, why "think step by step" was the discovery that started it, and when the thinking tax is worth paying. The core idea is almost embarrassingly mechanical once you have M5.7: a transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token — so the only way to compute more is to emit more tokens.

Why "think step by step" works: tokens ARE computation

Recall the shape of generation: each output token gets one forward pass — the same number of layers, the same matrices, whether the token is "the" or the final digit of a hard calculation. Ask for an instant answer to a multi-step problem and you're demanding the whole derivation be crushed into a single forward pass's worth of compute. It can't fit; the model pattern-matches a plausible answer instead. But let it write intermediate steps, and each step's tokens are appended to the context and attended to by everything after (M5.3) — the sequence becomes a scratchpad, partial results become inputs, and one pass per small step is plenty. Chain-of-thought isn't the model "explaining itself"; it's the model renting extra forward passes and using its own context window as working memory.

From trick to dial: test-time compute

For a decade the recipe for better answers was "train a bigger model" — spend more training compute (M7.4's scaling laws). The o1/R1 generation added a second axis: spend more inference compute on each hard question. Accuracy on math and code climbs smoothly as thinking budget grows — a scaling law over thinking tokens — before plateauing. Simplest version: self-consistency — sample N chains of thought at temperature (M11.1), take the majority answer; different chains make different errors, and voting averages them out (fifth appearance of ensemble logic in this course). Reasoning models internalize the whole loop: one long private chain that drafts, checks, backtracks — "wait, that's wrong, let me redo this" — before the visible answer.

How reasoning models are trained: RL on verifiable rewards

You can't SFT your way to real reasoning at scale — imitating written derivations (M8.3) teaches the style of reasoning, complete with confidently-wrong steps. The breakthrough (documented openly in DeepSeek-R1) is reinforcement learning against graders that can't be charmed: generate attempts at math and code problems, check them mechanically — the answer matches, the unit tests pass (M11.4's most-trusted evals, now used as reward) — and reinforce whatever token-habits preceded success. No human ranks anything (contrast RLHF, M8.4). Trained this way, models spontaneously develop longer chains, self-checking, and backtracking, because those behaviors win reward. The honest caveat: this works where verification is cheap (math, code) and transfers only partially to fuzzy domains — which is why reasoning models dominate benchmarks in exactly those two areas.

P(at least one correct) = 1 − (1 − p)ᴺ

In words: if one sampled chain solves the problem with probability p, then N independent tries fail only if all N fail. p = 0.4 and N = 8 gives 1 − 0.6⁸ ≈ 0.98 — near-certainty from a coin-flip model, IF you can recognize the right answer among the tries. Majority voting is that recognizer when answers are checkable; that little "if" is the entire reason verifiable domains got reasoning models first.

Real numbers

The self-consistency paper's headline: GSM8K accuracy for PaLM jumped ~18 points just by sampling 40 chains and voting — no new training. OpenAI's o1 reported AIME (competition math) going from ~12% (GPT-4o) to ~74–83% with internal thinking; DeepSeek-R1 published comparable jumps with the RL recipe in the open. The bill is equally real: a reasoning model may emit thousands of hidden thinking tokens per answer — at output-token prices (M7.5), a "quick question" can cost 10–50× the fast model. Latency follows: seconds → minutes.

Connect the dots

Every gear in this page is one you already own: fixed compute per token is M5.7's generation loop; attention over written steps is M5.3; sampling diverse chains is M11.1's temperature; verifiable rewards are M11.4's execution-based evals promoted from grader to teacher; the RL loop is M8.4 with a unit test where the human used to be. And the model picker in your tools finally has a policy: extraction, formatting, RAG answers over provided context → fast model; multi-step math, tricky code, planning → pay the thinking tax. Your RAG bot's typical query needs retrieval quality (M9.5), not reasoning tokens.

Build: measure CoT and self-consistency yourself
import re, collections
from openai import OpenAI
client = OpenAI()

PROBLEMS = [  # 10 GSM8K-style problems with known answers
    ("A shop sells pens at $3 for a pack of 2. How much for 14 pens?", 21),
    # ... 9 more
]

def ask(q, cot, T=0.7):
    suffix = " Think step by step, then give 'ANSWER: n'." if cot \
             else " Reply with ONLY 'ANSWER: n'."
    r = client.chat.completions.create(model="gpt-4o-mini", temperature=T,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": q + suffix}])
    m = re.search(r"ANSWER:\s*([\d.]+)", r.choices[0].message.content)
    return float(m.group(1)) if m else None

for label, fn in [
    ("direct",           lambda q: ask(q, cot=False, T=0)),
    ("chain-of-thought", lambda q: ask(q, cot=True,  T=0)),
    ("self-consistency", lambda q: collections.Counter(
        ask(q, cot=True, T=0.8) for _ in range(5)).most_common(1)[0][0]),
]:
    acc = sum(fn(q) == a for q, a in PROBLEMS) / len(PROBLEMS)
    print(f"{label:18s} {acc:.0%}")

Expected shape: direct < CoT < self-consistency, with the gap widest on multi-step problems — the accuracy-vs-compute curve, measured on your own ten problems for about a cent. Then note self-consistency cost 5× the tokens of CoT: you've rediscovered the thinking tax and can now decide when it's worth it.

If you remember one thing

A transformer spends identical compute on every token, so the only way to think harder is to emit more tokens — chain-of-thought turns the context window into working memory, sampling-and-voting turns diversity into accuracy, and reasoning models bake the loop in via RL against verifiable rewards (math checks, tests pass). The tax is real: pay it for multi-step reasoning, skip it for lookups, formatting, and well-retrieved RAG answers.

Interview questions

Mechanistically, why does chain-of-thought prompting improve accuracy?

Per-token compute is fixed: one forward pass through L layers regardless of difficulty, so a single-token answer to a multi-step problem must compress the whole derivation into one pass — infeasible, and the model substitutes pattern-matching. CoT lets the model emit intermediate results as tokens, which enter the context and are attended to by subsequent positions: the sequence becomes external working memory, and total computation scales linearly with tokens emitted. Each step is now a small, high-accuracy prediction. Two corollaries: CoT helps most on tasks needing sequential sub-results (math, logic, multi-hop QA) and barely at all on single-hop recall; and the "reasoning" text needn't be faithful to internal computation to be useful — it works by giving compute, not by being an honest self-report.

Explain self-consistency and when it beats a single greedy chain.

Sample N chains of thought at nonzero temperature, extract each final answer, return the majority. Rationale: distinct chains take distinct reasoning paths whose errors are (partly) independent, while correct paths converge on the same answer — so voting concentrates on truth like any ensemble; if a single chain succeeds with probability p, N tries contain a correct one with 1−(1−p)^N. It beats greedy when (a) the task has a short, comparable final answer to vote over, (b) single-chain accuracy is middling (p≈0.3–0.7 — the regime where votes flip outcomes), and (c) you'll pay N× tokens. It's useless for long open-ended outputs (nothing to vote on — you'd need a judge/reranker instead) and wasteful when p is already ~0.95.

How does reasoning-model training (R1-style) differ from RLHF, and why did it emerge in math and code first?

RLHF (M8.4) optimizes against a learned reward model trained on human preference rankings — expensive, subjective, and gameable (reward hacking: verbose, sycophantic outputs). R1-style training replaces the human/reward-model with mechanical verification: sample solutions, check the final answer against ground truth or run the unit tests, reward exactly the successes (plus format rewards), reinforce with policy-gradient methods. The grader is incorruptible and infinitely scalable, so the model can explore millions of attempts — and behaviors like long chains, self-verification, and backtracking emerge because they causally increase reward. It emerged in math/code because those domains have cheap, reliable verifiers; the open frontier is extending it to fuzzy domains via rubric-based or judge-based rewards, which reintroduces the gameability RLHF suffers.

Give your decision rule for routing between a fast model and a reasoning model in production.

Route by task structure and verifiability, not prestige. Fast model: retrieval-grounded answering (quality lives in the retriever, M9.5), extraction, classification, formatting, summarization, casual chat — reasoning tokens add latency and cost with near-zero accuracy gain. Reasoning model: multi-step quantitative work, non-trivial code generation/debugging, constraint-heavy planning, problems where you'd want to double-check your own work. Two practical patterns: escalation (fast model first; retry on the reasoning model when confidence is low, validation fails, or the user disputes) and budget capping (many APIs expose a thinking-effort knob — tune it on your golden set, M11.4, instead of maxing it). Track cost-per-resolved-query, not cost-per-token: a 20× pricier call that avoids three retries can win.

Is a model's chain-of-thought a faithful explanation of how it got its answer?

No — and this matters for anyone using CoT as an audit trail. The chain is generated text conditioned on the prompt, not a printout of internal activations; studies show models can produce correct answers with flawed chains, flawed answers with clean-looking chains, and post-hoc rationalizations — e.g., when a bias is injected into the prompt (the answer hinted as "(A)"), models often follow the hint while writing a chain that never mentions it. CoT's benefit is computational (more passes + working memory), which doesn't require faithfulness. Treat chains as a useful scratchpad and debugging signal, not as ground truth about the model's "reasons" — for real assurance, verify outputs externally (tests, checkers, grounding checks — the M11.4/12.3 toolkit).
