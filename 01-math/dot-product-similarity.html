Dot Product = Similarity

This page teaches one operation. It happens to be the operation your vector database runs on every query, the operation inside every neuron, and — when you reach Module 5 — the operation attention uses to decide which words look at which. Learn it once here, and you'll recognize it everywhere for the rest of the course.

Multiply-and-add. That's it.

The dot product of two vectors: multiply them slot by slot, add up the results. One number out.

a · b  =  a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + … + aₙbₙ
In plain words: line the two lists up, multiply each pair, sum everything. Two vectors go in, one number comes out — and that number measures agreement.

What the number means: agreement

Look at when the dot product gets big: when the two vectors have large values in the same slots with the same signs. Positive×positive adds, negative×negative also adds, mismatched signs subtract, and a zero contributes nothing. So the dot product is literally a score of slot-by-slot agreement.

Geometrically, the same fact looks like this: the dot product measures how much one arrow points along the other. Arrows pointing the same way → large positive. Perpendicular → zero (no agreement, no disagreement). Opposite ways → negative.

Cosine similarity: dot product with the lengths removed

One problem: the raw dot product also grows when a vector is simply longer (bigger numbers overall) — a loud vector would beat a relevant one. For comparing meanings we only care about direction. So we divide out both lengths:

cos(a, b)  =  (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)
In plain words: the dot product, after shrinking both arrows to length 1 so only their directions matter. ‖a‖ ("norm") is just the arrow's length: √(a₁² + a₂² + …). The result is always between −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (same direction).
Real numbers

With all-MiniLM-L6-v2: cos("How do I reset my password?", "Steps to reset your password") ≈ 0.88 · cos("How do I reset my password?", "Update your billing address") ≈ 0.31 · cos("How do I reset my password?", "Best pasta in Bengaluru") ≈ 0.04. Useful calibration: for this model, unrelated text usually scores 0.0–0.2, topically related 0.3–0.6, near-paraphrase 0.7+. There is no universal threshold — every model has its own score climate, which is why you tune cutoffs empirically.

Why cosine and not plain distance?

You could also compare vectors by Euclidean distance (straight-line distance between the points). For text embeddings, cosine is the default because embedding models mostly encode meaning in direction, while vector length can drift with text length and token statistics — noise you'd rather ignore. Bonus fact: MiniLM's vectors come out normalized (length 1). For unit vectors, cosine, dot product, and Euclidean distance all rank neighbors identically — so your vector DB can pick whichever is fastest (usually plain dot product) and nothing changes.

Every pair at once: the similarity matrix

Real systems rarely compare just one pair. Embed 5 sentences, and you can compute all 5×5 cosines at once — a similarity matrix, where cell (i, j) holds cos(sentence i, sentence j). The diagonal is all 1.0 (everything matches itself). Hold onto this picture: in Module 5, attention builds exactly this kind of every-token-versus-every-token score grid, and in Module 9, MiniLM's training loss is computed over exactly this kind of batch matrix. The similarity matrix is one of the three or four pictures this whole field runs on.

Connect the dots

"Semantic search", "vector similarity", "nearest neighbors", "relevance score" — across Pinecone, pgvector, FAISS, Chroma, Qdrant, it is all this one formula. When your RAG retrieval returns a chunk with score: 0.83, you now know the exact arithmetic that produced that number: multiply 384 pairs, add, divide by lengths. The mystique of your entire retrieval layer is one line of math.

Build: a vector search engine in 15 lines
import numpy as np
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

docs = [
    "Steps to reset your password",
    "Update your billing address",
    "How to delete your account",
    "Change your notification settings",
    "Best pasta in Bengaluru",
]
model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
D = model.encode(docs, normalize_embeddings=True)      # (5, 384)
q = model.encode(["I forgot my password"], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]

scores = D @ q            # 5 dot products at once = cosine (unit vectors!)
for i in np.argsort(-scores):
    print(f"{scores[i]:.3f}  {docs[i]}")

# sanity-check against the library's own implementation:
print(util.cos_sim(q, D))

The line scores = D @ q is a vector database's core loop — everything else (indexes, HNSW, sharding) is about doing this faster at scale, which we cover in M12. Try adding your own documents and watch the ranking respond.

If you remember one thing

Dot product = multiply-and-add = agreement score. Cosine = dot product with lengths removed = direction agreement. One number, computed between vectors, is how every system you use decides what's similar — and in Module 5 you'll watch a transformer use the very same number to decide which words should pay attention to which.

Interview questions

Explain cosine similarity to a non-ML engineer in two sentences.

Every text becomes an arrow in space, pointed according to its meaning. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two arrows: same direction ≈ 1 means same meaning, unrelated directions ≈ 0, opposite ≈ −1.

When do dot product and cosine similarity give the same ranking? Why does it matter?

When vectors are normalized to unit length — then a·b = cos(a,b) exactly, and Euclidean distance also produces the same neighbor ranking. It matters because most embedding models (including all-MiniLM with normalize_embeddings=True) output unit vectors, letting vector DBs use the cheapest metric without changing results. If vectors are not normalized, dot product favors longer vectors and the rankings can differ.

Why might raw dot product be a bad relevance score for unnormalized embeddings?

Because it conflates magnitude with agreement: a vector with big values scores high against everything, regardless of direction. A long, generic document could outrank a short, precisely relevant one. Normalizing (cosine) removes the length effect and compares pure direction.

Is a cosine similarity of 0.75 "good"?

Meaningless without context: score distributions are model-specific (and even domain-specific). 0.75 is near-paraphrase territory for all-MiniLM but might be common noise for another model. The right approach is empirical: look at the score distribution on your own data, or better, evaluate retrieval with labeled pairs (recall@k — M9) rather than trusting absolute score values.

What's the computational cost of cosine similarity between two d-dimensional vectors, and of searching n documents?

One pair: O(d) — d multiplies and adds. Brute-force search: O(n·d) per query. At n=1M, d=384 that's ~400M multiply-adds — a few tens of milliseconds on modern hardware, which is why brute force is fine up to a surprisingly large n; approximate indexes like HNSW (M12) exist for when it isn't.

Two sentences with no words in common — can they still score high cosine similarity?

Yes, and that's the whole point of embeddings: "The CEO stepped down" and "The chief executive resigned" share almost no tokens but land close in embedding space because the model learned meaning, not word overlap. TF-IDF/BoW would score them near zero — this is precisely the failure that pushed the field from counting to embeddings (M2).
