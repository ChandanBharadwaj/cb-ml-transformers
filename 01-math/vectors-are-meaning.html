Vectors Are Meaning

Every time you call model.encode("some text"), you get back a list of 384 numbers. This page is about what that list is. Not yet how it's computed — what it is. Because once you can picture a vector three ways at once, half of machine learning stops being mysterious.

A list of numbers = a point = an arrow

A vector is just an ordered list of numbers, like [3, 2]. But the same list can be pictured two more ways: as a point (go 3 right, 2 up, put a dot there) and as an arrow from the origin to that point. All three views are the same object — and ML people switch between them constantly without saying so. That silent switching is half of why the field feels confusing from outside.

384 dimensions (and why you don't need to picture them)

[3, 2] lives in 2-D. Add a number, it's 3-D. MiniLM's output [0.021, −0.413, 0.117, …] has 384 entries, so it's a point in 384-dimensional space. Nobody can picture that — not researchers either. And here's the liberating part: nobody needs to. Everything we do with vectors — measuring distance, comparing directions, adding them — is defined by formulas that work identically in 2 dimensions and 384. So we build intuition in 2-D, where we can see, and trust the formulas to carry it upward.

Why so many dimensions? Each one is a degree of freedom — roughly, one more axis of "ways texts can differ." Two dimensions can separate maybe topic and tone. To place all of language so that everything similar is nearby, you need room. 384 dimensions is room.

Real numbers

The actual start of all-MiniLM-L6-v2's vector for the word "coffee": [−0.038, 0.121, −0.041, 0.032, −0.083, 0.041, 0.088, −0.041, …] — 384 floats, each typically between −0.15 and 0.15 after normalization. Individually the numbers mean nothing readable. Only the whole pattern, compared against other vectors, carries meaning. That's normal and expected — meaning in embeddings is relational, not per-slot.

The big idea: nearby = similar

Here is the single most important sentence of this module:

The rule of the space

An embedding model's whole job is to place text in space so that similar meanings land close together and different meanings land far apart. Every downstream trick — search, RAG, clustering, deduplication, recommendation — is just geometry on top of that promise.

How long is a vector? Norms and normalization

Every arrow has a length — in ML jargon, its norm, written ‖v‖. It's just Pythagoras, run across all the slots:

‖v‖ = √(v₁² + v₂² + … + vₙ²)
In plain words: square every slot, add them up, take the square root — the straight-line distance from the origin to the point. For v = [3, 4]: √(9 + 16) = 5. Works identically in 384-D.

Why care? Because dividing a vector by its own norm gives a unit vector — same direction, length exactly 1. This is called normalization, and all-MiniLM does it to every embedding it outputs (that's the normalize_embeddings=True flag you may have seen). The effect: all your embeddings live on the surface of a sphere, so only direction differs between them. On the next page you'll see why that makes similarity comparisons both cheaper and fairer — it's a tiny detail that quietly makes your whole vector database behave sensibly.

Vector arithmetic: add, subtract, scale

Vectors support arithmetic, done slot-by-slot: [3, 2] + [1, −1] = [4, 1], and 2 × [3, 2] = [6, 4]. Geometrically, adding vectors means walking one arrow, then the other; subtracting a − b gives the arrow that takes you from b to a — it captures the difference between two meanings as a direction. Scaling stretches an arrow without turning it.

That subtraction fact is about to pay off.

king − man + woman ≈ queen

The most famous result in embedding history (from word2vec, 2013): take the vector for king, subtract man, add woman — and the nearest word to where you land is queen. Nobody programmed that. It emerged from training, and it tells you something profound: directions in the space carry meaning too. The arrow from man to king is approximately the same arrow as from woman to queen — a reusable "royalty" direction.

Connect the dots

When your RAG pipeline embeds a user's question and finds document chunks "about the same thing", it is doing exactly what the meaning-space animation showed: dropping a green point into the space and grabbing its neighbors. Your vector DB doesn't understand text at all — it does geometry on MiniLM's placements. Which means: retrieval quality is decided by where the embedding model places things, and everything else is plumbing. That's why M9 (how MiniLM learned its placements) will matter so much to your day job.

Build: see a meaning space with your own eyes

Make a toy 2-D meaning space and plot it — then make a real one:

# pip install sentence-transformers scikit-learn matplotlib
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

words = ["coffee", "tea", "espresso", "latte",
         "python", "pandas", "numpy", "laptop",
         "king", "queen", "man", "woman"]

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
vecs = model.encode(words)              # shape (12, 384)
xy = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(vecs)   # squash 384-D → 2-D

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 6))
plt.scatter(xy[:, 0], xy[:, 1])
for (x, y), w in zip(xy, words):
    plt.annotate(w, (x, y), fontsize=11)
plt.title("MiniLM's meaning space (2-D shadow)")
plt.show()

You should see the drinks huddle together, the code words huddle together, and the royal four form their own corner. You just verified the rule of the space on the exact model you use in production.

If you remember one thing

A vector is a list, a point, and an arrow at once. Embedding models place text into a high-dimensional space where closeness is similarity and directions are meanings — and every vector operation you'll ever meet in this course works the same in 384-D as in the 2-D pictures above.

Interview questions

What does it mean for two embeddings to be "close", and why is that useful?

Close means a small distance (or high cosine similarity — next page) between the vectors. Because the model was trained to place similar meanings nearby, geometric closeness becomes a computable proxy for semantic similarity — which turns "find related text" into "find nearest points", a fast, well-studied problem.

Why are embeddings hundreds of dimensions instead of 2 or 3?

Each dimension is a degree of freedom for expressing differences. Language varies along a huge number of axes (topic, tone, tense, formality, domain…), and low-dimensional spaces can't keep all similar things close while keeping all different things apart. Hundreds of dimensions give the model enough room; 384 (MiniLM) or 768–3072 (larger models) are engineering sweet spots between expressiveness, speed and storage.

Can you interpret an individual dimension of an embedding?

Generally no. Meaning is distributed across the whole vector, and the space is only defined up to rotation — training could produce a rotated version with identical behavior. Analysis tools sometimes find directions (not axes) that correlate with interpretable properties, like the royalty direction in the analogy example, but a single raw dimension is rarely meaningful on its own.

What does king − man + woman ≈ queen actually demonstrate?

That the embedding space has linear structure: consistent semantic relationships (gender, royalty, tense, country→capital) appear as roughly constant vector offsets. It demonstrates the space encodes relations, not just similarity. It's also worth knowing the caveat: this works impressively but imperfectly, and mostly for frequent words in static-embedding models.

You embed 1M documents with model A, then upgrade to model B. Can you compare A-vectors to B-queries?

No. Each model defines its own space — coordinates are meaningless across models (even across versions of the same model). Mixing them gives garbage similarities. Upgrading the embedding model means re-embedding the whole corpus; this is a real operational cost you plan for in RAG systems.
