Evaluating & Tuning Embeddings

The emotional payoff of the course. You know how your embedding model was built (9.1–9.4); this capstone page is where you measure it on your own data and make it better — the retrieval metrics, how to read MTEB without being fooled, and the fine-tune that typically buys +10–25 recall points on domain corpora for an hour of GPU time. This is the single most portfolio-worthy skill in the course.

The three retrieval metrics, animated once, used forever

Building the eval set from your own corpus

The metrics need (query, relevant-chunk) pairs from your domain — 50–200 is plenty to start. Three sources, in order of quality: real user queries from your logs, hand-matched to the chunk that answers them (an hour of honest work, the gold standard); synthetic queries — for each sampled chunk, have an LLM write 2–3 questions it answers (scales instantly; slightly optimistic since generated questions echo the chunk's wording); held-out FAQ/doc-title pairs if your corpus has them. Rules carried from M3.3/M8.2: keep this set sealed from any fine-tuning data, dedupe near-identical queries, and include the awkward real-world ones — misspelled, vague, one-word — because that's your production distribution.

Reading MTEB without being fooled

MTEB (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) ranks embedders across 50+ tasks — retrieval, clustering, classification, STS. Use it as a shortlist generator, not a decision, with three M3.6-grade cautions: (1) Task mismatch — the leaderboard average blends tasks you don't have; open the per-task columns and read only Retrieval scores for RAG. (2) The cost column is missing — #1 models are often 7B-parameter monsters (100× MiniLM's cost per embedding; remember 9.4's triangle) and the top-10 spread can be ~3 points; check size and dimensionality (storage!) next to the score. (3) Contamination and overfitting-to-the-benchmark (M3.3's experimenter-leakage at field scale) — newer models are partly tuned toward MTEB. The professional loop: shortlist 2–3 plausible models from MTEB Retrieval → run YOUR recall@k eval → decide with your numbers.

The fine-tune: MNR on your pairs

And now everything converges. Take your (query, chunk) pairs — the training split, not the sealed eval! — and run 9.3's exact loss on 9.2's towers with M8.2's recipe. MiniLM is 22M parameters: full fine-tuning, no LoRA needed, minutes per epoch on any GPU. Why it works so well: generic embedders spread capacity over all of language; your corpus lives in a narrow slice full of domain vocabulary, product names, and question styles the generic geometry treats as noise. A few hundred pairs teach the space what "similar" means in your world — M8.1's transfer-learning miracle, applied to geometry.

Real numbers: typical before/after

Representative results for fine-tuning all-MiniLM-L6-v2 on 150–300 domain pairs (widely reproduced pattern): general-domain corpora: recall@5 0.82 → 0.85 (modest — the base model already knows general English). Technical/product corpora (your case): 0.61 → 0.79. Jargon-heavy niches (legal, medical, internal tools): 0.45 → 0.72. The rule: the further your domain from Reddit-and-Wikipedia English, the bigger the win. Cost: under an hour on a T4; inference cost after: identical (same architecture, same 384 dims — only the geometry moved).

Build (MODULE CAPSTONE): fine-tune YOUR retriever, measure the jump
from sentence_transformers import (SentenceTransformer, InputExample,
                                   losses, evaluation)
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader

# 1) your pairs: (real or synthetic query, the chunk that answers it)
#    from your RAG corpus. 150+ train pairs, 50+ SEALED eval pairs.
train_pairs = [("how do I reset my password",
                "Open Settings > Security > Reset Password. A link arrives by email."),
               # ... yours here ...
              ]
eval_queries = {"q1": "I forgot my login"}
eval_corpus  = {"c1": "Open Settings > Security > Reset Password...", }  # all chunks
eval_relevant = {"q1": {"c1"}}                                           # truth

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

# 2) BEFORE score — never skip the baseline (M3.6!)
ir = evaluation.InformationRetrievalEvaluator(
    eval_queries, eval_corpus, eval_relevant,
    accuracy_at_k=[5], name="before")
print("recall@5 before:", ir(model))

# 3) the fine-tune: 9.3's loss, 8.2's recipe
train_data = [InputExample(texts=[q, c]) for q, c in train_pairs]
loader = DataLoader(train_data, shuffle=True, batch_size=32)   # 31 free negatives
loss = losses.MultipleNegativesRankingLoss(model)
model.fit(train_objectives=[(loader, loss)], epochs=2,
          warmup_steps=int(len(loader) * 0.1), show_progress_bar=True)

# 4) AFTER — same sealed eval
print("recall@5 after:", ir(model))
model.save("my-domain-minilm")     # drop-in replacement: same dims, same speed

Then the honest extras: 3 seeds (M8.2), a skim of the failures that remain (they seed your next data round — and your hard negatives, 9.3), and one regression check that general queries didn't degrade (M8.1's forgetting, geometric edition). Re-embed your corpus with the saved model and your production RAG just got measurably better — with a before/after number for the portfolio.

If you remember one thing

Evaluate retrievers with recall@k on your own sealed (query, chunk) pairs — MTEB shortlists, your eval decides. Then fine-tune with MNR on a few hundred domain pairs: an hour of compute for double-digit recall gains whenever your domain strays from generic English. The model you've used blindly for years is now something you can audit, explain, and improve on demand.

Interview questions

Define recall@k, MRR and nDCG, and match each to its use case.

recall@k: fraction of queries whose relevant item appears in the top k — the RAG feeding metric (set k to your retrieval top-k). MRR: mean of 1/rank-of-first-relevant — rewards rank-1 placement; right for top-answer UIs and reranker evals. nDCG@k: sums graded relevance with logarithmic position discounts, normalized by the ideal ranking — the general search metric when relevance isn't binary. Interviewers want the mapping, not just definitions: "did we retrieve it" vs "did we rank it first" vs "how good is the whole ordered page".

How would you build an evaluation set for a RAG retriever with no labeled data?

Best: mine real user queries from logs, hand-annotate the answering chunk(s) for 100–200 of them. Scalable: synthetic generation — sample chunks, have an LLM produce 2–3 questions each chunk answers (caveat: lexical echo makes scores optimistic; paraphrase or use a different model to roughen them). Supplement with existing structure (FAQ pairs, ticket→doc links). Discipline: seal it from training data, dedupe, include messy real-world queries, and version it — it's your regression suite (M12.3 extends it to full-pipeline eval).

Why does fine-tuning an embedder help so much on domain data, and when does it NOT?

Helps: generic contrastive training (9.3) defined "similar" over internet-general pairs; domain corpora have vocabulary, entities and query-styles that geometry treats ambiguously — a few hundred domain pairs re-carve the space where it matters (transfer learning on geometry; gains scale with domain-distance from generic text: +3 points on general corpora, +20+ on jargon-heavy ones). Doesn't help: when retrieval already saturates (recall@5 > 0.9 — errors are elsewhere), when failures are chunking/query-ambiguity problems (M12.1/12.2 territory), or with too few/dirty pairs (noise in, geometry damage out). Diagnose before tuning: inspect WHERE recall fails first.

How do you use the MTEB leaderboard responsibly?

As a shortlist filter: sort by the RETRIEVAL task family (not the blended average), then weigh the unlisted columns — parameter count/latency (7B embedders cost ~100× MiniLM per call), embedding dimensionality (storage and search cost scale with it), max sequence length, license. Assume some benchmark-tuning/contamination in top entries (the M11.4 skepticism). Final decision only from your own recall@k on your corpus — leaderboard deltas of 2–3 points routinely invert on specific domains. "Shortlist globally, decide locally."

After fine-tuning your embedder, what must change operationally in the RAG system?

Re-embed and re-index the ENTIRE corpus — old and new vectors live in different geometries and cannot be mixed (M1.1's cross-model rule; fine-tuning made it a different model). Plan: versioned indexes, dual-write or blue-green swap, and re-run the eval suite against the new index before cutover. Also version the model artifact with the index it built. Forgetting this — querying an old index with new-model query vectors — silently degrades results and is a classic postmortem; mentioning it unprompted is a strong production signal.
