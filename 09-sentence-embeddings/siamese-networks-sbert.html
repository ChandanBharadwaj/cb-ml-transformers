Siamese Networks & SBERT

2019. BERT dominates every benchmark — including sentence-pair similarity, where you feed it both sentences at once and it classifies their relationship brilliantly. So why did anyone need sentence-BERT? Because of an arithmetic catastrophe hiding in that "both at once" — one that makes BERT-style comparison architecturally incompatible with search. This page is that catastrophe, the twin-tower fix, and the reason your RAG system can pre-embed documents at all.

The catastrophe: pair-wise reading doesn't scale

BERT's native similarity mode is the cross-encoder: concatenate [CLS] sentence A [SEP] sentence B [SEP], run the full encoder, let attention compare every word of A against every word of B (M5.3's machinery at its best), output one relationship score. Maximally accurate — and the score exists only for that pair. Nothing reusable is produced. Now count what search needs: your query against 1M chunks = 1M full encoder passes per query. Clustering 10k sentences = 50M passes. The accuracy is perfect; the economics are impossible.

The trade, stated honestly

The bi-encoder pays for its speed: each text is embedded blind to what it'll be compared against, so subtle query-document interactions (negation alignment, which entity matches which) are compressed into 384 numbers before the question is known. Cross-encoders, reading pairs jointly, catch what pre-compression loses — typically worth several accuracy points on ranking benchmarks. The production resolution you already sense coming: use both — bi-encoder recalls candidates from millions, cross-encoder re-scores the top 50 (reranking, M12.2). Cheap wide net, expensive fine net.

Symmetric vs asymmetric: a detail that bites RAG builders

One subtlety with direct production impact: what counts as "similar" depends on the task. Symmetric search matches like with like (duplicate questions, paraphrases). Asymmetric search matches a short query against a long answer-bearing passage — "how do I reset my password?" should land near a paragraph that never contains those words but answers them. Models are trained for one or the other (all-MiniLM: general/symmetric-leaning from its pair diet; e5/bge: asymmetric-aware, using prefixes like "query: …" / "passage: …" to tell the towers which role a text plays). Using a symmetric model for question→document retrieval — or forgetting a prefix-trained model's prefixes — quietly costs recall. Check the model card; it's a one-line fix.

Real numbers

The SBERT paper's headline: clustering 10,000 sentences needed ~65 hours of cross-encoder BERT passes vs ~5 seconds with siamese embeddings + cosine — a 46,000× speedup that created the sentence-embedding field. The accuracy trade today: on retrieval benchmarks, a MiniLM-class bi-encoder alone gets ~85–90% of the ranking quality of a cross-encoder over the same corpus; the two-stage combo (M12.2) recovers nearly all of it at ~1% of the cost.

Connect the dots

Read your own RAG pipeline against this page: index-time encode() over your corpus = the right tower, run offline; query-time encode() = the left tower; your vector DB's top-k = the dot-product sweep. You built a siamese architecture without the name. And M4.4's promise — "attention is RAG inside a sentence" — now closes from the other side: your RAG is a bi-encoder approximation of cross-attention, with the reranker (M12.2) buying back what the approximation loses. One mechanism, three zoom levels.

Build: measure the trade yourself
import time
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, CrossEncoder, util

docs = ["To reset your password, open Settings and choose Security.",
        "Our refund policy allows returns within 30 days.",
        "The API rate limit is 100 requests per minute.",
        "Two-factor authentication adds a second login step."] * 250  # 1,000 docs
query = "I forgot my login credentials"

bi = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
cross = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2")

t0 = time.perf_counter()
D = bi.encode(docs, normalize_embeddings=True)          # offline in real life!
q = bi.encode([query], normalize_embeddings=True)
bi_scores = (D @ q.T).ravel()
t_bi = time.perf_counter() - t0

t0 = time.perf_counter()
cross_scores = cross.predict([(query, d) for d in docs])
t_cross = time.perf_counter() - t0

print(f"bi-encoder   : {t_bi:.2f}s (and encoding is one-time!)")
print(f"cross-encoder: {t_cross:.2f}s for the SAME 1,000 comparisons")
print("bi top hit   :", docs[bi_scores.argmax()][:60])
print("cross top hit:", docs[cross_scores.argmax()][:60])

Expect the cross-encoder to take 20–60× longer for 1,000 docs — then imagine a million. Both find the password doc here; on harder queries the cross-encoder's rankings are subtly better — which is exactly the gap M12.2's reranking stage exists to harvest.

If you remember one thing

Cross-encoder = read the pair together: most accurate, nothing reusable, O(n) full passes per query. Bi-encoder (siamese towers, SBERT) = embed each text alone with shared weights: precomputable vectors, cosine comparison, search over millions in milliseconds — the architectural fact that makes RAG possible. The trade is real, and two-stage retrieval buys it back. Next page: the loss that teaches the towers where to put things.

Interview questions

Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder — the canonical retrieval question.

Bi-encoder: two weight-shared encoder passes embed each text independently; similarity = cosine of the vectors. Enables offline indexing and millisecond search over millions (score = dot product), at the cost of compressing each text before knowing its counterpart. Cross-encoder: one pass over the concatenated pair with full cross-attention; several points more accurate for ranking, but produces no reusable representation — O(candidates) full passes per query. Production pattern: bi-encoder retrieves top-k, cross-encoder reranks (M12.2).

Why does weight-sharing between the towers matter?

Both texts must land in the SAME space for cosine to mean anything — separate weights would give two different geometries with no comparable coordinates (M1.1's "no cross-model comparison" rule, inside one system). Sharing also halves parameters and lets one deployed model serve both index-time and query-time encoding. (Asymmetric variants keep shared weights but add role prefixes — same space, role-aware placement.)

What information does a bi-encoder fundamentally lose vs a cross-encoder?

Pair-specific interaction: each text is compressed to a fixed vector before the comparison partner is known, so fine-grained alignments — which entity corresponds to which, negation scope against the query's intent, multi-constraint matching — are only preserved if the training data taught the model to pre-encode them. Cross-attention computes these interactions explicitly per pair. That's why cross-encoders win rankings and why hard queries (negations, multi-hop constraints) are bi-encoder weak spots.

Symmetric vs asymmetric semantic search — and a production bug it explains.

Symmetric: both sides are the same kind of text (duplicate/paraphrase detection). Asymmetric: short query vs long answer-bearing passage — similarity means "answers", not "resembles". Models are trained for a mode; asymmetric-trained ones (e5, bge) often require role prefixes ("query: ", "passage: "). Bug: deploying such a model without the prefixes (or a symmetric model for QA retrieval) silently degrades recall — no errors, just worse ranking. Check the model card's usage section; it's the cheapest recall improvement available.

Your corpus grows from 10k to 10M chunks. What breaks in each architecture?

Cross-encoder-only search was already impossible (10M passes/query). Bi-encoder: encoding cost scales linearly but is offline/parallel (M7.1 batching); query-time brute-force dot products go from trivial (10k×384 ≈ 4M ops) to heavy (~4B ops, tens-to-hundreds of ms) — this is exactly the point where ANN indexes (HNSW, M12.1) replace brute force, trading tiny recall for 100× latency. Memory: 10M × 384 floats ≈ 15 GB — quantized/PQ-compressed vectors enter the conversation. Knowing WHERE each cost bites is the systems-maturity signal.
