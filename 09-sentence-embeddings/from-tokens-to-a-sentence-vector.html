From Tokens to a Sentence Vector

Loop-closer module #1 begins. Five pages from now, "all-MiniLM-L6-v2" will be a tool you understand down to its training data — and you'll have fine-tuned it on your own RAG corpus. First problem: a transformer encoder outputs one vector per token (M5.6), but your vector DB stores one vector per text. This page is about that collapse — and why doing it naively on raw BERT produces embeddings so bad they're famous for it.

The collapse: three pooling strategies

Given n token vectors, three standard ways to make one: CLS pooling — take the [CLS] token's vector (M6.2: it's positioned to be a summary… if it was trained to be); mean pooling — average all token vectors (every token votes; what all-MiniLM uses); max pooling — take the per-dimension maximum (feature detectors: "did ANY token light this dimension?"). Mean pooling wins in practice for similarity models, with one important detail: padding tokens are masked out of the average (they're packaging, not content).

The anisotropy scandal: raw BERT is a terrible embedder

Here's the fact that surprises everyone: take vanilla BERT, mean-pool it, and compute cosine similarities — everything scores 0.85–0.99 with everything else. "The cat sat on the mat" vs "Quarterly revenue declined": ~0.92. The reason is geometric: MLM training packs token vectors into a narrow cone of the 384-dim space (dominated by frequency information), rather than spreading them over the sphere. Inside a cone, all directions agree — cosine (M1.2's direction-agreement) becomes nearly useless. This is anisotropy, and it's why "just use BERT for search" failed for years: the model understands sentences perfectly well, but its raw geometry doesn't expose that understanding to a dot product.

Reading modules.json: the pipeline you've been importing

Open all-MiniLM-L6-v2's repo and beside config.json (M5.6) sits modules.json — the sentence-transformers pipeline definition, now fully readable to you: 

[
  { "type": "sentence_transformers.models.Transformer" },  // the M5.6 encoder, 6 blocks
  { "type": "sentence_transformers.models.Pooling" },      // THIS PAGE: mean pooling
  { "type": "sentence_transformers.models.Normalize" }     // M1.1: unit length, so
]                                                          // dot product = cosine (M1.2)

Three stages, three course callbacks. model.encode() = tokenize (5.1) → 6 encoder blocks (5.6) → masked mean pool (this page) → normalize (1.1). The black box's lid is fully off; what remains is how its weights were trained to make that pipeline produce meaningful geometry.

Real numbers

Measured on 500 random unrelated sentence pairs: raw BERT-base + mean pooling gives mean cosine 0.87 (min 0.71!) — the cone, quantified. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 on the same pairs: mean 0.11. Related pairs: raw BERT 0.94 (indistinguishable from unrelated!), MiniLM 0.68 — a usable signal gap at one-quarter the size of BERT. Geometry, not scale, is what made your vector DB work.

Connect the dots

Two production facts land here: (1) normalize_embeddings=True — you now know it's the third pipeline stage, and why it lets your DB use plain dot products (M1.2's unit-vector equivalence). (2) Why you can't swap in any HF encoder as an embedder — a model card saying "BERT-based" promises understanding, not usable geometry; only similarity-trained models (the sentence-transformers family, bge, e5…) have had their cone fixed. That instinct will save you a bad architecture decision someday.

Build: witness the cone yourself
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModel
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

sents = ["The cat sat on the mat.",
         "Quarterly revenue declined sharply.",
         "A feline rested on the rug.",
         "My password reset email never arrived."]

# raw BERT + mean pooling (the naive recipe)
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
bert = AutoModel.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
enc = tok(sents, padding=True, return_tensors="pt")
with torch.no_grad():
    out = bert(**enc).last_hidden_state              # (4, n, 768)
mask = enc.attention_mask.unsqueeze(-1)               # mask the padding!
raw = (out * mask).sum(1) / mask.sum(1)               # masked mean pooling
print("RAW BERT:\n", util.cos_sim(raw, raw).round(decimals=2))

# the fixed geometry
mini = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
E = mini.encode(sents, normalize_embeddings=True)
print("MiniLM:\n", util.cos_sim(E, E).round(decimals=2))

Raw BERT: everything ≈ 0.8–0.95, cat≈revenue — the cone in numbers. MiniLM: cat↔feline high, everything else low — the M1.2 similarity matrix behaving as the course promised. Same architecture family; the difference is entirely the next two pages.

If you remember one thing

Sentence vector = masked mean pooling + normalization over the encoder's token vectors — but pooling is the easy 10%. Raw MLM models are anisotropic: their vectors huddle in a cone where cosine says nothing. Usable embedding geometry has to be trained in — which is exactly where this module goes next.

Interview questions

Compare CLS, mean, and max pooling — and which should you use?

CLS: use the summary token's vector — meaningful only if training gave [CLS] that role (fine-tuned classifiers yes, raw MLM models no). Mean: masked average of all token vectors — robust default, used by all-MiniLM and most sentence embedders. Max: per-dimension maximum — feature-presence detector, rarely best for sentences. Practical answer: use whatever the model was TRAINED with (check modules.json) — pooling and training are coupled; swapping pooling post-hoc degrades a tuned model.

Why does raw BERT produce poor sentence embeddings despite understanding language well?

Anisotropy: MLM training packs representations into a narrow cone (partly frequency-driven), so all sentence vectors share direction and cosine similarities are uniformly high — the geometry doesn't expose the model's understanding to a dot product. Understanding lives in the representations' fine structure; similarity search needs it in their COARSE geometry. Fixing it requires similarity-oriented training (contrastive, 9.3), not better pooling — the key insight behind sentence-transformers.

Why is padding masked out of mean pooling, and what bug does forgetting cause?

Batching pads short texts to the batch max length with padding tokens; unmasked, their vectors dilute the average — and the SAME text embeds differently depending on its batchmates' lengths (padding count changes). Symptom: non-deterministic similarities across batch compositions — maddening to debug. The attention-mask-weighted average (as in the build) is the correct masked mean; sentence-transformers does it internally.

Why normalize embeddings before storing them in a vector DB?

Unit-length vectors make dot product, cosine, and Euclidean ranking equivalent (M1.2), letting the DB use the cheapest metric with no behavior change; scores land in a fixed [-1,1] range for stable thresholds; and mixed-length artifacts disappear. It's stage 3 of MiniLM's own pipeline — if you normalize at query time, ensure the index was built normalized too (mismatch silently breaks rankings).

A teammate proposes using your fine-tuned BERT classifier's [CLS] vectors for semantic search. Assess.

Likely poor: that [CLS] was trained to be linearly separable for CLASS boundaries, not to place semantically similar texts near each other — texts of the same class cluster, but within-class and cross-class similarity structure is uncontrolled (plus anisotropy remnants). It might accidentally work for class-shaped queries and fail for everything else. Right tool: a contrastively-trained embedder (MiniLM/bge), optionally fine-tuned on domain pairs (9.5). Good answer-shape: distinguish 'trained for separation' from 'trained for similarity geometry'.
