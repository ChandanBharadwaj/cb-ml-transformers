Negative Sampling

Last page hid a scandal. We said the model turns a dot-product score into "the probability that these words co-occur" — but a probability over what? Over the whole vocabulary. And making that honest is so expensive it would have killed word2vec. The fix — negative sampling — is one of the great cheap tricks in ML, and (this is why the page matters to you) it is the direct ancestor of how all-MiniLM was trained. Learn the physics here once; M9 will feel like a rerun.

The problem: an absurdly expensive denominator

To claim "P(sat | cat) = 0.89" properly, the model must score every word and normalize — a softmax over the vocabulary (full treatment in M3.2). With 100,000 words, every single training pair needs 100,000 dot products just for its denominator. Multiply by ~100 billion training pairs, and the arithmetic reads: never finishes.

The reframe: from "pick the right word" to "spot the fake"

Negative sampling changes the question. Instead of "which of 100k words is the neighbor?" (multi-class, needs the giant denominator), it asks a yes/no question: "is this particular pair real or fake?" For each true pair (cat, sat), the trainer also fabricates k fake pairs by sampling random words — (cat, taxation), (cat, snorkel), … — and the model must answer yes to the real one and no to the fakes. Binary classification, k+1 dot products, done.

maximize  log σ(vcat·usat) + Σk fakes log σ(−vcat·ufake)
In plain words: σ squashes a score into 0–1 (meet it properly in M3.1). First term: push the real pair's agreement UP. Second: push each fake pair's agreement DOWN. Two forces, k+1 dot products, no vocabulary-wide anything.

Choosing the fakes: the ¾ trick

One subtlety with real consequences: which random words become negatives? Sample uniformly and you mostly pick ultra-rare words — too easy to reject, weak gradient signal. Sample by raw frequency and "the/of/and" dominate — also useless, since they co-occur with everything. word2vec's empirical sweet spot: sample proportional to frequency^0.75. The exponent squashes the distribution — frequent words get dampened, rare words get boosted — landing in the middle where negatives are plausible enough to be informative.

Generalize that lesson, because it outlives word2vec: the quality of contrastive learning is decided by the quality of its negatives. Too-easy negatives teach nothing; too-hard (actually-positive) negatives teach lies. In M9 this reappears as "hard negative mining" for sentence embedders, and in M12 it explains why your retriever's fine-tuning data needs carefully chosen wrong-answers. One knob, fifteen years of relevance.

Real numbers

The word2vec paper's recipe: k = 5–20 negatives for small datasets, k = 2–5 for huge ones (more data = each pair needs less squeezing). Under frequency^0.75 with real English statistics, a word like "the" (~7% of all tokens) drops to ~2.2% of negative draws, while a word appearing once per million tokens roughly triples its draw rate. Same-shaped tricks appear in modern LLM training data curation — down-weight the common, up-weight the rare.

Connect the dots

Here's the thread to hold: word2vec pulls word pairs together; all-MiniLM pulls (question, answer) and (sentence, paraphrase) pairs together; your RAG system works because queries were pulled toward the chunks that answer them. Three scales, one physics. Even GLiNER (M10) rides it: entity types and text spans pulled into a shared space. When M9 shows you "MultipleNegativesRankingLoss", you'll recognize it instantly: it's this page where the fakes come free from the batch instead of being sampled.

Build: feel the negatives knob
from gensim.models import Word2Vec
from gensim.utils import simple_preprocess

sentences = [simple_preprocess(l) for l in open("corpus.txt", encoding="utf-8")]

for k in [1, 5, 15]:
    m = Word2Vec(sentences, vector_size=100, window=5, sg=1,
                 negative=k, min_count=5, epochs=10, seed=42)
    print(f"\n--- negative={k} ---")
    print([w for w, _ in m.wv.most_similar("king", topn=5)])

With k=1 the space is usually mushy (nothing pushed apart hard enough); k=5–15 sharpens neighborhoods noticeably. You're not tuning an abstract hyperparameter — you're adjusting how hard the "push" force works, and you can see the space tighten as you raise it.

If you remember one thing

Negative sampling replaces an unaffordable "compare against everyone" with an affordable "beat a few plausible fakes" — and the resulting pull-real/push-fake dynamics are the founding physics of contrastive learning, the training family behind word2vec, sentence-transformers, CLIP, and your retriever. Cheapest idea in this course per unit of consequence.

Interview questions

Why is the full softmax over a vocabulary too expensive for word2vec, and what does negative sampling change?

Softmax needs a normalizing sum over all |V| words for every training example — O(|V|) dot products per pair, ruinous at 10⁵ vocab × 10¹¹ pairs. Negative sampling reframes multi-class prediction as binary discrimination: score the true pair plus k sampled fake pairs (k+1 dot products), pushing the real up and fakes down. Cost drops from O(|V|) to O(k) with negligible quality loss for representation learning.

Why are negatives sampled with frequency^0.75 rather than uniformly or by frequency?

Uniform sampling over-picks rare words — trivially rejectable, uninformative gradients. Raw frequency over-picks stopwords — which legitimately co-occur with everything, so pushing them away is noise. The 0.75 power interpolates: common words dampened, rare words boosted, keeping negatives plausible-but-wrong, which is where the learning signal is strongest. It was found empirically, and the principle (negative difficulty matters) generalizes to all contrastive learning.

What is contrastive learning, and name systems beyond word2vec that use it.

Learning representations by pulling positive pairs together and pushing negative pairs apart in embedding space, rather than predicting labels. Examples: sentence-transformers / all-MiniLM (question–answer and paraphrase pairs), CLIP (image–caption pairs), SimCLR (two augmentations of one image), retrieval models like DPR/E5 for RAG. The negatives' quality and count are the key hyperparameters in all of them.

What happens if your "negative" samples are secretly positives?

You push apart things that belong together — direct damage to the geometry (false repulsion). It's a real problem at scale: sample randomly from a corpus and occasionally you'll draw a genuine paraphrase or true co-occurrence as a "fake". Mitigations: filter near-duplicates, use in-batch negatives from diverse sources, or losses more tolerant of label noise. In interviews this is the classic follow-up — it tests whether you understand the mechanism rather than the recipe.

Sketch how you'd apply negative sampling to train a retrieval model for RAG.

Positives: real (query, relevant-chunk) pairs — from click logs, QA data, or synthetic queries generated per chunk. Negatives: other chunks — random ones for cheap signal, plus hard negatives (chunks BM25 or the current model ranks high but that are wrong) for sharp decision boundaries. Train a bi-encoder with a contrastive loss so query vectors land near their chunks. This is literally the M9 capstone, and it's a strong interview answer because it's word2vec's idea wearing production clothes.
