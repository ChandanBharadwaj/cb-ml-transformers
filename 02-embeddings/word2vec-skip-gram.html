word2vec: Skip-Gram Internals

You've said "I know word2vec conceptually" — it turns words into vectors, similar words end up close. This page replaces conceptually with mechanically. By the end you'll know exactly what the training data is (spoiler: it's free), what the model is (two matrices — that's all), and what one training step physically does. Nothing here is beyond M1 math.

Step 1: free training data from a sliding window

word2vec's genius move is self-supervision: instead of needing labeled data, it invents a prediction game whose answers are already in the text. The game: given a word, predict its neighbors. Slide a window over every sentence ever written; each (center word, context word) pair is one training example.

Why does predicting neighbors teach meaning? Because of last page's distributional hypothesis: cat and kitten occur near the same neighbors (purr, fur, vet, sat). To predict those shared neighbors well, the model is forced to give cat and kitten similar vectors. Similar contexts → similar prediction targets → similar vectors. The geometry of meaning falls out as a side effect of winning the game.

Step 2: the model — two matrices, nothing else

The entire word2vec "network" is two matrices (M1.3 pays off here):

  • V (center embeddings): |vocab| × 300 — row i is word i's vector when it plays the center role.
  • U (context embeddings): |vocab| × 300 — row j is word j's vector when it plays the context role.

A forward pass for the pair (cat, sat): look up cat's row in V, dot it with sat's row in U, convert the score to a probability. That's it — one lookup, one dot product. (The "lookup" is officially a one-hot times the matrix — which just selects a row. Even one-hot found a job: it's the index card, not the meaning.)

score(center, context) = vcenter · ucontext
In plain words: "how strongly does the model currently believe these two co-occur?" — measured by the M1.2 agreement score between their two vectors. Train the matrices so real pairs score high, and closeness-in-space = shares-contexts = similar-meaning becomes literally true.

Skip-gram vs CBOW (the two flavors)

Everything above is skip-gram: center predicts context. The mirror version, CBOW (continuous bag of words), averages the context vectors and predicts the center. CBOW trains faster (one prediction per window instead of several); skip-gram squeezes more signal out of rare words (each rare center gets several training examples per appearance, and no averaging dilutes it). Practical rule everyone quotes: skip-gram for quality, CBOW for speed — and skip-gram with the next page's trick is the configuration that made word2vec famous.

What the trained matrix actually is

After training, matrix U is usually discarded, and V — the center-embedding matrix — is "the word vectors". When you loaded pretrained word2vec or GloVe vectors back in the day, you were loading exactly this: one matrix, one learned row per word. The famous king−man+woman geometry from M1.1 lives in these rows — nobody designed it in; it condensed out of billions of tiny pull-closer nudges.

Real numbers

The classic Google News word2vec release (2013): vocabulary 3 million words and phrases × 300 dimensions, trained with skip-gram on ~100 billion words. That's a 3M × 300 matrix ≈ 900M parameters — bigger than BERT (110M)! But it's a shallow lookup table, not a deep network: enormous memory, almost zero computation. Deep models flipped that trade: fewer parameters doing far more compute per token. Keep this contrast — it returns in M7 when we count FLOPs vs bytes.

Connect the dots

Look again at what training did: pull vectors of things that belong together closer; the geometry becomes meaning. Now recall what your RAG stack assumes: question vectors land near answer-chunk vectors. Same principle, bigger units — all-MiniLM (M9) was trained by pulling sentence pairs together the exact way word2vec pulls word pairs together. You are two pages away from understanding the training objective (negative sampling) that both share. Also: the "embedding table" you just met never went away — it's literally the first layer of every transformer, including GPT-4's (M5.2).

Build: train word2vec yourself, inspect the space
# pip install gensim
from gensim.models import Word2Vec
from gensim.utils import simple_preprocess

# any text you like — a book from Project Gutenberg, your docs, chat exports
sentences = [simple_preprocess(line) for line in open("corpus.txt", encoding="utf-8")]

model = Word2Vec(
    sentences,
    vector_size=100,   # dimensions
    window=5,          # the sliding window's reach
    sg=1,              # 1 = skip-gram, 0 = CBOW  ← you know what this means now
    negative=10,       # next page's trick
    min_count=5,
    epochs=10,
)

print(model.wv.most_similar("king", topn=8))
print(model.wv.most_similar(positive=["king", "woman"], negative=["man"]))  # the analogy
print(model.wv["king"][:10])   # 10 of the 100 learned numbers — your own embedding

Two experiments worth the time: (1) train on a tiny corpus vs a big one and watch neighbor quality change — data volume is the quality knob; (2) try window=2 vs window=10: small windows learn syntactic neighbors (cat↔cats), big windows learn topical ones (cat↔veterinarian). You're tuning the definition of "context" and watching meaning shift with it.

If you remember one thing

word2vec = a self-made prediction game (guess the neighbors) + two matrices + M1's dot product and gradient descent. Meaning is never taught; it precipitates out of billions of co-occurrence nudges. "I know word2vec conceptually" has now become: lookup → dot → probability → nudge. That's the whole machine.

Interview questions

Explain skip-gram's training objective in one paragraph.

For each word in a corpus, take its surrounding words within a window. Train embeddings so the dot product between a center word's vector and each true context word's vector yields a high co-occurrence probability, updating both vectors by gradient descent when predictions are wrong. Words sharing contexts converge to nearby vectors — the distributional hypothesis made mechanical.

Why does word2vec use two embedding matrices instead of one?

A word's behavior as a center differs from its behavior as a neighbor — e.g. a word rarely co-occurs with itself, which one shared matrix would struggle to express (it would push a vector away from itself). Two roles, two matrices, cleaner optimization. After training, typically only the center matrix V is kept (or the two are averaged).

What does self-supervised mean, and why was word2vec a landmark example?

The supervision signal is manufactured from the input itself — here, neighboring words are the "labels" — so no human annotation is needed and the dataset scales to all text ever written. word2vec was an early proof that self-supervision on raw text learns real semantics; masked-LM (BERT) and next-token prediction (GPT) are the same idea with deeper models. In a real sense modern LLM pretraining is word2vec's game, scaled up.

Skip-gram vs CBOW — differences and when to prefer each?

Skip-gram: center predicts each context word — slower (multiple predictions per window) but stronger on rare words and small datasets. CBOW: averaged context predicts center — faster, slightly better for frequent words. Default answer: skip-gram + negative sampling for quality; CBOW when training speed on a huge corpus dominates.

How does the window size hyperparameter change what's learned?

Small windows (2–3) capture syntactic/functional similarity — words swappable in-place (walk/walking, cat/cats). Large windows (10+) capture topical relatedness — words from the same scene (doctor/hospital/nurse). Neither is "correct"; it defines what counts as context, i.e., what kind of similarity your space encodes. Good interviewers love this question because it shows whether you understand that embeddings reflect their training choices.

Where do word2vec-style embedding tables appear inside modern transformers?

As the input embedding layer: a |vocab| × d matrix whose rows are looked up by token id — structurally identical to word2vec's V. Differences: it's trained jointly with the whole network (not as a standalone objective), operates on subword tokens, and its outputs get transformed by attention into contextual representations. In MiniLM that table is ~11.7M of the 22.7M parameters.
