Properties & Limits of Embeddings

You now know how static word embeddings are made. This page is the audit: what actually lives inside a trained space (some of it wonderful, some of it ugly), the two siblings you'll hear about in interviews (GloVe, fastText), and the fatal flaw that ended the static-embedding era — setting up everything from Module 4 onward.

What lives in the space

Clusters: neighborhoods of related words — you saw them in M1.1 and built them yourself with gensim. Directions: consistent offsets encoding relations — gender, tense, country→capital. The king−man+woman walk works because "royalty" and "gender" are roughly fixed arrows anywhere in the space. Frequency artifacts: very common and very rare words sit in odd regions — the space encodes corpus statistics, not just semantics.

And bias. Embeddings are compressed corpus statistics, and corpora are written by humans. The infamous finding (Bolukbasi et al., 2016): in word2vec trained on Google News, man − woman ≈ computer programmer − homemaker. Nothing "learned to be biased" — the pull/push physics faithfully compressed biased co-occurrences into geometry. For an engineer the lesson is operational: whatever regularities your corpus contains, your embeddings will serve back at query time — including ones you'd rather they didn't. Debiasing exists (projecting out a gender direction) but is shallow; bias re-emerges from indirect associations. Know it, measure it in your domain, don't claim it away.

The siblings: GloVe and fastText

GloVe (Stanford, 2014) reaches nearly identical spaces by a different route: instead of streaming windows, it first builds the global co-occurrence count matrix, then factorizes it so that dot products match log co-occurrence counts. Prediction (word2vec) vs counting-then-compressing (GloVe) — later shown to be near-equivalent mathematically. Interview one-liner: "word2vec is online and predictive; GloVe is batch and count-based; the resulting geometries are similar."

fastText (Facebook, 2016) fixes a real word2vec hole: unknown words. It represents each word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors — "kitten" = <ki + kit + itt + tte + ten + en> + … So a typo ("embedings") or a brand-new word still gets a decent vector assembled from familiar pieces, and morphology comes free (walk/walking/walked share most n-grams). Hold this thought: sharing sub-word pieces solves unknown words — that's the same insight that becomes BPE tokenization in every modern LLM (M5.1).

Real numbers

Ask classic word2vec (3M-word vocab!) for "embedings" — typo, one letter missing — and you get a KeyError: not in vocabulary, no vector, full stop. fastText assembles one from n-grams and its nearest neighbors come back as embeddings, embedding, vectors… Exactly the difference between a filing cabinet with fixed drawers and a system that understands pieces. GloVe's standard release: 400k vocab, 300-D, trained on 42B tokens of Common Crawl.

The fatal flaw: one word, one vector, forever

Now the ceiling. A static embedding assigns each word exactly one point in space — permanently. But words don't have one meaning:

  • "I sat by the river bank."
  • "The bank raised interest rates."

word2vec must place "bank" somewhere. Trained on both kinds of sentences, it lands on a compromise — a point between river-country and money-country that is the right answer for neither sentence. Every polysemous word (and most words are) gets this blurry average treatment. No amount of more data fixes it; the representation itself lacks a slot for "which meaning, right now?"

Why this page is the hinge of the course

Every limitation listed above has the same root: the vector is computed before the sentence is known. Static embeddings are a dictionary; meaning is a conversation. The fix requires a machine that reads the whole sentence and lets words adjust each other — "bank" noticing "river" nearby and sliding toward water. That machine is attention, and the architecture built around it is the transformer. Modules 3–5 construct it piece by piece; you now know precisely which problem it was built to kill.

One more dot to connect: all-MiniLM outputs contextual embeddings — that's why it embeds whole sentences meaningfully at all. The 384 numbers you get for "the bank raised rates" already contain the disambiguation this page showed was impossible for word2vec. What you've been calling "an embedding model" is a transformer whose whole job is solving the bank problem, 6 layers deep.

Connect the dots

Practical checklist this page gives you: (1) If your RAG corpus is full of product IDs and rare exact terms, remember fastText's lesson — subword/hybrid approaches beat pure semantics there. (2) If retrieval behaves oddly around ambiguous words ("java", "apple", "python"), you're watching the bank problem — contextual embeddings mitigate it but the query may still be one ambiguous word with no disambiguating context. This is exactly why one-word queries perform worst in your vector search, and why query expansion (M12.2) exists.

Build: catch the bank problem red-handed
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util
m = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

s = ["I sat by the river bank this morning",
     "The bank raised interest rates today",
     "The boat drifted toward the muddy shore",
     "My loan application was approved by the lender"]

E = m.encode(s, normalize_embeddings=True)
print(util.cos_sim(E, E).round(2))
# river-bank ↔ shore scores HIGH; money-bank ↔ lender scores HIGH;
# river-bank ↔ money-bank scores LOW — same word "bank", different vectors.
# A static embedding could never produce this matrix.

That low score between the two "bank" sentences is your proof that MiniLM computes meaning-in-context. Keep this snippet — when M5.3 shows you the attention weights that produced it, run it again and the whole pipeline will be transparent.

If you remember one thing

Static embeddings put every word at one fixed address — brilliant for word-level similarity, structurally incapable of handling words whose meaning depends on their sentence. The rest of this course builds the machine that computes a fresh vector per word per sentence — and every tool you use daily, MiniLM included, is that machine.

Interview questions

Static vs contextual embeddings — the canonical question. Answer it well.

Static (word2vec/GloVe/fastText): one fixed vector per word, computed once at training, identical in every sentence — fails on polysemy ("bank"). Contextual (BERT/MiniLM/GPT hidden states): the vector for each token is computed fresh from the entire input via attention, so the same word gets different vectors in different sentences. Static = a dictionary lookup; contextual = a function of the sentence. Everything post-2018 that matters is contextual.

Compare word2vec, GloVe and fastText in three sentences.

word2vec learns embeddings by predicting context words with a sliding window (online, predictive). GloVe builds a global co-occurrence matrix first and factorizes it so dot products match log-counts (batch, count-based) — resulting spaces are similar. fastText extends word2vec with character n-gram vectors summed per word, handling out-of-vocabulary words, typos and morphology that the other two simply cannot represent.

How can word embeddings be biased, and what would you do about it in production?

Embeddings compress corpus co-occurrence statistics, so social stereotypes present in the text become geometric regularities (the programmer/homemaker offset). In production: measure it on your task (e.g., check retrieval/classification behavior across demographic terms in your domain), prefer task-specific evaluation over generic debiasing claims, and treat "projecting out a bias direction" as a partial mitigation — indirect associations survive it. The honest answer is measurement + monitoring, not a one-shot fix.

Why can't more training data fix the polysemy problem for static embeddings?

Because it's a representational limit, not a data limit. The model's output type is one vector per word; a word with two meanings needs two (or a context-dependent) representation. More data just refines the position of the compromise point between the meanings — the compromise itself is baked into the architecture. Fixing it required changing what's computed: a function of (word, sentence) instead of (word).

Your vector search behaves badly on short, ambiguous queries. Explain why, using this module's concepts.

Contextual embedders disambiguate using surrounding context — and a one-word query has none. "java" alone gets an averaged, genre-blurred vector (coffee? island? language?), so its neighbors span all senses. Documents, being longer, are well-disambiguated — the mismatch is on the query side. Mitigations: query expansion/rewriting (add context words), asking the user's intent, hybrid sparse+dense scoring, or conversation history as context. This is the bank problem operating in your production system.
