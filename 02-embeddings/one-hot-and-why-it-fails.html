One-Hot & Why It Fails

This module answers a question you've carried for years: how does a machine learn what words mean? You already know the pre-embedding world — bag-of-words, TF-IDF, counting. This page shows precisely where that world hits its ceiling, because the failure is what makes word2vec's fix (next page) feel inevitable instead of magical.

From counting to coordinates

Your TF-IDF experience already treats text as vectors: one dimension per vocabulary word, values from counts. Push that idea to its purest form and you get the one-hot vector: to represent a single word, take a vector as long as the whole vocabulary, put a 1 in that word's slot, 0 everywhere else. It's the representation lurking underneath every count-based method — each document vector in bag-of-words is just a sum of one-hots.

The two fatal flaws

Flaw 1 — size. One dimension per word means real vocabularies produce absurd vectors: 50,000 words → 50,000-dimensional vectors that are 99.998% zeros. Everything downstream (storage, multiplication, learning) pays for dimensions that almost never activate.

Flaw 2 — orthogonality (the deep one). Every pair of one-hot vectors has dot product 0. In M1's language: all words are mutually perpendicular arrows — cat is exactly as unrelated to dog as it is to pizza. The representation cannot express "these two words are similar" at all. There is no geometry of meaning; there's just an arbitrary filing cabinet. This is why your TF-IDF search engine can't tell that "CEO resigned" matches "chief executive stepped down": at the word level, zero overlap means zero score.

Real numbers

A 50,000-word vocabulary: each one-hot vector stored naively as float32 is 50,000 × 4 bytes = 200 KB per word. A word2vec-style dense vector: 300 × 4 bytes = 1.2 KB — 167× smaller, while carrying vastly more information about meaning. (In practice one-hots are stored sparsely, but the moment they enter a neural layer they become dense — the cost is real.) The efficiency gap is nice; the meaning gap is the revolution.

The idea that fixes it: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps"

Linguist J.R. Firth wrote that line in 1957, and it became the distributional hypothesis: words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings. You never see a definition of tezgüino, but read "a bottle of tezgüino", "tezgüino makes you drunk", "we brewed tezgüino from corn" — and you know it's an alcoholic drink. The contexts are the meaning.

This is the crucial pivot, because context is countable from raw text. No dictionary, no labels, no human annotation — just a mountain of sentences, which the internet supplies for free. If similar contexts ⇒ similar meaning, then a representation built from contexts will place similar words close together. That's exactly the "rule of the space" from M1, derived from a 1957 insight.

What we actually want

Stating the target precisely — because next page's algorithm is engineered to hit each point:

  • Dense: most slots non-zero, every dimension doing work — not 99.998% zeros.
  • Low-dimensional: 100–1000 dims, not vocabulary-sized. Forces the model to compress, and compression forces generalization.
  • Similarity-preserving: cos(cat, dog) high, cos(cat, pizza) low — geometry that mirrors meaning.
  • Learned from raw text: no labels, so it scales to billions of words.
Connect the dots

Every limitation you ever hit with TF-IDF search — synonyms scoring zero, plural vs singular mismatches, "no results" for a perfectly reasonable rephrasing — was Flaw 2 wearing different costumes. And notice something about your own career arc: you moved from TF-IDF to calling all-MiniLM without ever seeing what happens in between. This module is that in-between. By its end, "dense retrieval" vs "sparse retrieval" (terms you'll meet in every RAG article) will read as: geometry-of-meaning vs filing-cabinet — and you'll know hybrid search (M12) combines them because each catches what the other misses.

Build: watch one-hot fail, in four lines
import numpy as np

vocab = ["cat", "dog", "pizza", "runs", "the"]
onehots = np.eye(len(vocab))          # identity matrix = all one-hots (M1 callback!)

# every pairwise similarity:
print(onehots @ onehots.T)
# → the identity matrix again: 1s on the diagonal, 0 everywhere else.
#   Translation: every word matches itself and nothing else.

Note the neat coincidence: the matrix of all one-hot vectors is M1's identity matrix, and its similarity matrix is also the identity — the "no structure" matrix. Compare that with the blocky similarity matrix you computed for real sentences in M1.2: those blocks are exactly what one-hot can never produce.

If you remember one thing

One-hot representations make every word perpendicular to every other — similarity is structurally impossible, not just unmeasured. The escape route is the distributional hypothesis: meaning lives in context, context is countable, and counting it lets geometry replace the filing cabinet.

Interview questions

What's the difference between one-hot encoding and an embedding?

One-hot: a sparse, vocabulary-sized vector with a single 1 — hand-assigned, carries only identity, all pairs orthogonal (similarity always 0). Embedding: a dense, low-dimensional, learned vector positioned so that semantically similar items are geometrically close. One-hot says "which word"; an embedding says "what it means relative to everything else."

Why is the orthogonality of one-hot vectors a problem for NLP?

Because it hard-codes the assumption that all words are equally unrelated. Any model consuming one-hots must learn everything about word relationships from scratch for each word separately — knowledge about "cat" transfers zero to "kitten". Embeddings let related words share statistical strength, which is why they massively improved sample efficiency across all of NLP.

State the distributional hypothesis and explain why it matters practically.

"Words occurring in similar contexts have similar meanings." Practically, it converts meaning — an unlabeled, subjective thing — into co-occurrence statistics that can be computed from unlimited raw text with no human labeling. It's the theoretical license for word2vec, BERT's masked-word pretraining, and ultimately GPT-style next-token prediction: all of them learn meaning purely from context.

Is TF-IDF completely obsolete then?

No — and saying so in an interview is a mistake. TF-IDF/BM25 (sparse retrieval) is exact about rare tokens: IDs, error codes, product SKUs, names — precisely where embeddings are weakest (they smooth everything toward meaning). Modern search often runs hybrid: sparse for exact-term precision, dense for semantic recall, scores fused. Knowing when counting beats geometry is a senior-engineer signal.

Word embeddings are ~300-D, one-hots are ~50,000-D. Why does shrinking dimensions add information?

The compression is the teacher. With only 300 dimensions for 50k words, the model cannot give every word private space — it's forced to share dimensions, and the only efficient sharing is putting similar words in similar regions. Information about relationships gets created because identity-only storage no longer fits. (Same principle returns in autoencoders and in why LLM hidden sizes matter, M7.)
